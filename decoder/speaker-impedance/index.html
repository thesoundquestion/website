LESSON 04 · SPEAKERS · IMPEDANCE & MATCHING

Why doesn't an 8-ohm speaker stay at 8 ohms?

Read speaker impedance curves, nominal ohms, and amplifier load requirements.

WHAT THE EXAMPLE STAT ACTUALLY MEANS

8 Ω nominal · minimum 6.4 Ω

Illustrative, not a verified product measurement.

Read every part of the label

The 8 Ω figure is a nominal designation, not a constant value. A minimum of 6.4 Ω means the magnitude is stated not to fall below 80% of nominal in the rated band. That relationship appears in IEC 60268-5. A typical impedance plot may rise dramatically at a driver's resonance without implying louder sound at those frequencies.

What it doesn't establish: The rating tells you neither the phase angle nor how much current your specific amplifier can safely deliver. This fictional curve illustrates the specification, not a tested speaker.

Lower impedance demands more current

At the same voltage, a 4 Ω test load draws twice the current of an 8 Ω load. The amp’s power supply and output circuitry have to handle the extra current. For a real speaker, look at how low its impedance dips.

THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

How the electrical load changes with the music

Impedance measures opposition to alternating electrical current. A speaker has moving voice coils, crossover components and resonances, so its impedance changes with the frequency of the signal. The single number on the carton is shorthand for the load an amplifier should expect.

Look at the ohm value for the note you’re playing. By Ohm’s law, fewer ohms draw more current at the same voltage. Compare how two amps handle a 4 Ω load to see why their 8 Ω wattage ratings only tell part of the story. A real speaker’s electrical phase also affects how much power it receives.

When two different speakers are equally loud and their amplifiers are operating comfortably, the nominal 4 Ω or 8 Ω labels do not define the sound. The meaningful risk is exceeding a particular amplifier's stable operating range.

WHAT MIGHT YOU HEAR?

Connect the measurements to listening

An amplifier may sound clean with a demanding loudspeaker at modest levels, then approach clipping, protection or excess heating when a loud passage requires more current. That is a system interaction, not proof that low impedance inherently sounds worse.

Bass tightness, separation and musical dynamics cannot be read directly from nominal impedance. Speaker response, sensitivity, distortion, placement and the amplifier's actual capabilities all play roles.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“Eight-ohm speakers are easy to drive” and “four-ohm speakers have more powerful bass” are common shortcuts. A nominal label alone doesn't establish either conclusion.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

Impedance magnitude and phase, minimum impedance, listening level and amplifier current/voltage capacity all influence matching. A nominal 8 Ω speaker can still present surprisingly challenging dips. IEC 60268-5 includes a rated-impedance minimum-magnitude convention.

USE THIS WHEN BUYING

What matters in practice?

  1. Check the rated load supported by the amplifier, and look for minimum impedance rather than only nominal impedance.
  2. Consider sensitivity and listening distance: a nominal rating doesn't tell you how loudly the speaker will play for a given input.
  3. Avoid connecting multiple speakers in parallel without understanding how their combined load affects the amplifier.

Other important variables

Phase angle, speaker sensitivity, amplifier thermal limits and listening distance.

Engineering deep dive Optional

For a pure resistor only, I = V/R. At 20 V RMS, 8 Ω draws 2.5 A and 4 Ω draws 5 A. Loudspeakers are complex frequency-dependent loads, so the instantaneous relationship also depends on phase.
