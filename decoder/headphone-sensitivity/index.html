LESSON 11 · HEADPHONES

Will your source drive your headphones?

Read sensitivity and impedance together to see what a headphone asks of your dongle or amplifier. The same printed sensitivity can mean different things when the test units change.

EXAMPLE PRODUCT SPECIFICATION 300 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS

DECODE THE EXAMPLE

300 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS

Ideal calculation for a 300 Ω resistive load with linear acoustic response.

What the rating tells you

300 Ω is the nominal load. 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS is the sound pressure level for one volt across that load in the example test. RMS means root mean square, the effective voltage for the calculation. At 300 Ω, 1 V supplies 3.33 mW. The same sensitivity works out to about 96.8 dB SPL at 1 mW, which takes 0.548 V RMS.

The 32 Ω example also prints 102 dB SPL, but its reference is 1 mW. Convert both to a shared reference before comparing. Treat these levels as test references only.

Same sound pressure, different electrical demands

All three fictional examples use the same 90 dB SPL test-tone reference and ideal resistive loads. Each bar keeps its scale when you switch headphones.

Showing: Efficient 32 Ω
Calculated values. Each row has its own units and scale. Compare voltage to voltage, current to current and power to power across cases.

Efficient 32 Ω

A small output can go a long way

Rated 32 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 mW. This example reaches the shared reference with about 45 mV RMS and 1.40 mA RMS.

Sensitive headphones and IEMs can reach the required level with modest power. Low background noise is worth checking because hiss may be easier to hear.

300 Ω

This load needs more voltage

Rated 300 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS. The same reference takes about 251 mV RMS and 0.84 mA RMS.

It asks for the most voltage and the least current of the three. A source with limited voltage swing can run out of room for peaks.

Low-sensitivity 16 Ω

This load asks for more current

Rated 16 Ω · 86 dB SPL at 1 mW. The reference takes about 200 mV RMS and 12.53 mA RMS.

It takes less voltage than the 300 Ω example and about fifteen times as much current. The source needs to sustain that current into the lower load.

Match the units to the question

dB SPL at 1 V is handy when you know the source’s clean voltage into a headphone load. dB SPL at 1 mW is handy when you know the power into that load. The nominal impedance lets you estimate one from the other.

Low impedance draws more current at the same voltage. Higher impedance can demand more voltage at the same power. The sensitivity tells you how much electrical drive is needed for a given acoustic level, so the actual limit depends on both numbers.

Allow for music’s peaks when comparing with a source’s clean-output limits. For a linear headphone on a fixed resistive load, a signal segment 10 dB higher calls for about 3.16 times the voltage and current, and ten times the power.

What happens when a source reaches its limit?

If the source reaches its voltage or current limit, louder peaks can distort or compress. Hiss in quiet passages points to the noise floor of the source and how sensitive the headphone is to it.

A clean source with sufficient output can already cover the range you use. For changes in tonal balance between sources, check source output impedance as well.

COMMON CLAIM

“High-ohm headphones are always harder to drive.”

WHAT TO CHECK

Compare the voltage and current needed for the same test level. In the chart, 300 Ω asks for the highest voltage, and the less sensitive 16 Ω example demands the most current and power.

What should you look for?

  1. Check whether sensitivity is quoted for 1 mW or 1 V, and look for the test frequency or method.
  2. Find the source’s clean output into a load near your headphone impedance. Read the load and distortion conditions attached to the rating.
  3. Check peak margin and noise. Extra available voltage helps when voltage is the limit, and low hiss matters with sensitive IEMs.
Engineering deep dive Optional

For a resistive load R, P = V²/R and I = V/R, using RMS voltage and current. If sensitivity is quoted in dB SPL at 1 mW, add 10 log₁₀(1000/R) to estimate dB SPL at 1 V RMS. If sensitivity is quoted at 1 V, subtract that same term to estimate dB SPL at 1 mW.

In the 300 Ω example, 10 log₁₀(1000/300) ≈ 5.23 dB. Manufacturer values can use different fixtures, test frequencies and averaging. Use the impedance at the test frequency when it’s available.
