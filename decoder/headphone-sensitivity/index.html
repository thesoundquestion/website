LESSON 11 · HEADPHONES

Will your headphones play loud enough?

Your headphone jack needs enough output to reach the volume you want and handle sudden peaks. Two headphone specs tell you what the jack has to supply.

Sensitivity
How loud the headphones play with a given amount of electrical input.
Impedance
How much the headphones oppose current. At the same voltage, lower ohms draw more current.

DECODE THE EXAMPLE

300 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS

Find the dot marked 1 V

300 Ω is the headphone’s stated impedance. The gold dot shows that applying 1 V RMS produces 102 dB SPL in the example test. RMS is the effective voltage of the test signal. SPL means sound pressure level.

The blue dot shows the same headphone with one milliwatt of power. That takes about 0.548 V RMS and gives about 96.8 dB SPL in this example.

The 32 Ω example also prints 102 dB SPL. Read what follows “at” to see which input each rating uses. These are test levels.

A little voltage is enough

This 32 Ω headphone is rated 102 dB SPL at 1 mW. It reaches the test level with 45 mV and 1.40 mA.

A sensitive headphone like this can play loudly from a modest source. Listen for hiss between tracks if you use sensitive IEMs.

Check the jack’s voltage limit

This 300 Ω headphone is rated 102 dB SPL at 1 V RMS. It needs 251 mV and 0.84 mA for the same test level.

It needs the most voltage of the three. A jack that can supply only a small voltage may run out of room on loud peaks.

Check how much current the jack can supply

This 16 Ω headphone is rated 86 dB SPL at 1 mW. It needs 200 mV and 12.53 mA to reach the test level.

It needs a little less voltage than the 300 Ω example and about fifteen times its current. Check the jack’s clean output into a load near 16 Ω.

Why do lower ohms draw more current?

Ohm’s law gives the current when you divide volts by ohms. Apply 1 V to a 64 Ω load and about 16 mA flows. Apply the same 1 V to 32 Ω and about 31 mA flows. Halving the resistance doubles the current in this example.

Sensitivity answers the other part of the question. It tells you how much input a headphone needs to reach a given sound level. That is why the 300 Ω example needs the most voltage and the 16 Ω example needs the most current at the same test level.

Listen for rough peaks and hiss

Try music you know at your usual volume. If the source runs out of voltage or current on a drum hit, the peak can sound rough or squashed. Hiss in quiet passages can come from the source’s noise, especially with sensitive headphones.

If you hear a change in bass or treble between jacks at the same listening level, see what the jack’s output impedance can change.

COMMON CLAIM

“High-ohm headphones are always harder to drive.”

What should you look for?

  1. Read the input next to the sensitivity rating. Compare headphones at the same reference, whether that is 1 V or 1 mW.
  2. Look up the jack’s clean output into a load close to your headphone’s ohm rating.
  3. Leave some room for loud peaks at your usual listening volume. Check for hiss if you use sensitive IEMs.
Convert sensitivity and calculate peak needs Optional

For a resistive load R, P = V²/R and I = V/R, using RMS voltage and current. If sensitivity is quoted in dB SPL at 1 mW, add 10 log₁₀(1000/R) to estimate dB SPL at 1 V RMS. If sensitivity is quoted at 1 V, subtract that same term to estimate dB SPL at 1 mW.

In the 300 Ω example, 10 log₁₀(1000/300) ≈ 5.23 dB. Manufacturer values can use different fixtures, test frequencies and averaging. Use the impedance at the test frequency when it’s available.

For a linear headphone on a fixed resistive load, a signal segment 10 dB higher calls for about 3.16 times the voltage and current, and ten times the power. See how volts and amps determine watts.
