LESSON 09 · HEADPHONES · DESIGN & FIT

How do headphone cups and fit change what you hear?

Look at the back of the cup to understand leakage. Look at the pad or tip to understand where the headphone sits and how it seals.

DECODE THE EXAMPLE

32 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 mW

Illustrative calculation for a linear, fictional headphone.

One reference point under a test condition

32 Ω is the nominal electrical load. 102 dB SPL at 1 mW means the headphone produces a sound-pressure level of 102 dB with one milliwatt of electrical input in the example test. A tenfold power change moves the idealized line by 10 dB.

Treat 102 dB SPL as the test reference only. Use the design description for cup and fit information. Use the headphone sensitivity lesson to compare ratings in dB per milliwatt and per volt.

What is behind the driver?

Compare the grille, vents and solid back. The examples all use over-ear pads so you can focus on the enclosure.

Open-back

The grille leaves the rear of the driver acoustically open. Outside sound reaches you readily, and people nearby can hear some of your music.

Some listeners enjoy the sense of space in a quiet room. Tuning, the recording and your ear shape also influence that impression.

Closed-back

A solid rear shell forms a chamber behind the driver. It typically reduces leakage and blocks more outside noise. A closed design can still use small tuning vents.

Pad fit and internal damping affect the response. Glasses, hair or worn pads can leave a gap that reduces bass on some models.

Semi-open

This example uses a partly vented rear cup. Manufacturers use “semi-open” for different amounts of acoustic venting, so leakage and isolation vary by model.

Check a specific model’s isolation and leakage if you share a room or record next to a live microphone.

Illustrations show representative constructions. The arrows indicate a sound path, with leakage varying by model.

Where does the pad or tip sit?

Over-ear and on-ear headphones can each have open or closed backs. In the two pad cutaways, blue shows the cushion and the gold dots mark where it touches.

What moves the air?

Dynamic drivers move a diaphragm with a coil. Planar magnetic drivers use conductive traces spread across a diaphragm in a magnetic field. Balanced-armature drivers, common in IEMs, use a tiny armature to move a diaphragm. Electrostatic headphones use a charged diaphragm and generally need dedicated drive electronics.

Some IEMs combine driver types and use crossovers to divide the frequency range. Driver choice influences how the device can be built and tuned. Listen to and compare measurements of the finished headphone to evaluate the result.

What might change when you put them on?

Room noise and sound leakage are often the clearest differences between open and closed cups. Tuning and how the headphone sits on your ears also shape the sense of space.

If an IEM suddenly loses low bass, try a comfortable tip that seals. If an over-ear model sounds thinner with glasses on, reseat the pad and compare at the same volume. The response and fit lesson shows that kind of bass change on a graph.

COMMON CLAIM

“More IEM drivers means more detail.”

WHAT TO CHECK

Several drivers give a designer more ways to divide the work. Crossovers, acoustic paths, tuning and fit determine what reaches the ear.

What should you look for?

  1. Pick the enclosure based on how much room noise and leakage you can live with.
  2. Choose a pad or tip that fits comfortably for a full listening session. Include glasses if you wear them.
  3. Use sensitivity and impedance together to check the drive requirement. For sensitive IEMs, also check the source for audible hiss.
Engineering deep dive Optional

For an ideal resistive load, P = V²/R. If the driver stays linear, a power ratio P/P₀ changes sound pressure level by 10 log₁₀(P/P₀) dB. The sample 32 Ω headphone uses about 0.179 V RMS to draw 1 mW. An actual headphone’s impedance can vary with frequency.
