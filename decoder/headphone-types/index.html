Open-back, closed-back or IEM: how should you choose?

Compare open-back, closed-back and in-ear designs, and decode impedance and sensitivity.

FIRST, DECODE THE EXAMPLE

32 Ω · 102 dB SPL at 1 mW

Illustrative construction or idealized calculation; not a real product measurement.

What each part means

32 Ω is the nominal headphone impedance. 102 dB SPL at 1 mW means the manufacturer claims that one milliwatt of electrical input produces a 102 dB sound-pressure level under its test conditions. The marked point in the graph shows exactly that reference. The other two points illustrate the theoretical 10 dB change for a tenfold power change.

What it doesn't establish: That specification does not identify whether the headphones are open, closed, planar or IEMs. It also doesn't describe frequency response, fit, maximum undistorted output or safe listening levels.

Open-back

The back of the earcup is acoustically open

Air and sound can pass through the back of the cup more easily. Isolation is limited and music is audible to people nearby, but the acoustic design can avoid some enclosed-cup resonances.

Open-backed headphones can feel more spacious to some listeners, yet that sensation also depends on tuning, ear geometry and recording.

Open does not automatically mean more accurate.

Closed-back

Isolation and bass depend on the seal

The earcup encloses the back of the driver and usually provides more isolation. Pads, clamping force and the cup's internal damping can strongly affect the frequency response.

Well-designed closed backs can deliver outstanding clarity and bass control; leaks around glasses or worn pads may reduce bass substantially.

Closed does not automatically mean boomy or lower fidelity.

IEM

A miniature design that seals the ear canal

IEMs sit in or at the ear canal and use ear tips to make a seal. A poor seal can dramatically change bass output. Dynamic drivers, balanced armatures and hybrids are common.

Multiple drivers can split the frequency range, but crossovers and acoustic paths introduce complexity. More drivers do not necessarily equal better sound.

Fit, nozzle shape, insertion depth and source noise can matter more than driver count.

BEGINNER FRIENDLY, ENGINEERING INFORMED

Construction, drivers and electrical matching answer different questions

Open-back and closed-back describe what happens behind the driver, while IEM describes a device that seals the ear canal. They are not an accuracy ranking. Open designs usually leak sound and isolate poorly. Closed designs often isolate better but are sensitive to cup geometry and pad seal. IEMs can provide substantial isolation, with results highly dependent on ear-tip fit.

Dynamic drivers use a moving coil, planar magnetics use a diaphragm driven over a wider surface, balanced-armature drivers are common in small in-ear designs, and electrostatics use a charged diaphragm that typically requires dedicated driving electronics. These designs have distinct engineering trade-offs, not automatic signatures of quality.

Impedance and sensitivity address how demanding the headphones are for the source. High impedance alone does not mean poor efficiency. A high-impedance model may need more voltage; a low-impedance, low-sensitivity model can demand significant current. Very sensitive IEMs may reveal background hiss from a noisy source.

THE LISTENING CONNECTION

What could you actually hear?

Open-back models may create a spacious presentation, while closed backs offer isolation from noise and keep music from disturbing others. The recorded soundstage, actual tuning and personal ear geometry also matter.

An IEM with a poor seal can lose bass even when its published frequency response looks impressive. Multi-driver designs may sound excellent, but more drivers can also introduce crossover and acoustic-path challenges.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“Planar is automatically faster,” “electrostatic is best,” and “more IEM drivers means more detail” are familiar but unproven shortcuts. A preference for a particular presentation is real; technology labels don't prove the source of that preference.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

Fit, measured frequency response, sensitivity, impedance, noise, distortion, channel matching and the device's acoustic coupling affect performance. The differences among designs must be evaluated through the finished product.

CHOOSING REAL EQUIPMENT

What should you look for?

  1. Choose open, closed or in-ear based first on isolation, leakage, comfort and your listening environment.
  2. Check sensitivity units: dB SPL for 1 mW is not the same reference as dB SPL for 1 V. Read it together with impedance.
  3. For IEMs, prioritize fit, tip seal, low source noise and output-impedance matching over driver count.

Other variables in the chain

Pads, insertion depth, seals, headphone amplifier voltage/current limits, output impedance, acoustic resonances and individual hearing.

Engineering deep dive Optional

For an ideal resistive headphone, P = V²/R and sound-pressure level changes roughly 10 log₁₀(P/P₀) dB, while the driver remains linear. A headphone specified at 102 dB SPL for 1 mW can be modelled at about 92 dB SPL for 0.1 mW and 112 dB for 10 mW, but these are theoretical levels, not safe listening recommendations or a measured device response.
