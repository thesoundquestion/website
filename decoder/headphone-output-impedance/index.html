LESSON 12 · HEADPHONES

Why can two headphone jacks sound different?

Plug the same headphones into two jacks and one may play quieter at the same setting. Sometimes the bass balance changes too. The jack’s output impedance helps explain both.

Headphone impedance
The headphone’s opposition to current. It can change from one note to the next.
Jack output impedance
How much the signal voltage drops inside the jack for a given current. A larger value means a larger drop.

Why does the voltage drop?

In the example, the current passes through both the jack’s internal resistance and the headphone. Together they act like two resistors in one path. Ohm’s law says voltage drop equals current times resistance.

At the same starting voltage, fewer total ohms means more current. That current passes through the jack’s own resistance, so more voltage drops inside the jack and less reaches the headphone. In the two-note example, that happens on the midrange note.

Real headphone impedance can vary with frequency. The 16 Ω or 32 Ω number on a box is a starting point. A measured impedance curve shows how the load changes across the notes.

Match the volume and listen to the balance

Use the same headphones, track and EQ settings. Match the listening level first. If the jack loses the same proportion of voltage at every frequency, the level changes evenly. A loss that varies by note can shift the bass, mids or treble.

COMMON CLAIM

“A higher-impedance headphone jack always sounds warmer.”

WHAT TO CHECK

Look at the headphone’s impedance curve. A high point in the bass can make the bass relatively stronger on some jacks. A high point elsewhere can change a different part of the sound.

What should you look for?

  1. Look up the jack’s output impedance and the headphone’s ohm rating. A small jack impedance relative to the headphone’s helps preserve the headphone’s balance.
  2. Look for a measured headphone impedance curve if available, especially with multi-driver IEMs. Large swings in ohms give the jack more opportunity to change the balance.
  3. Check whether the jack can supply enough clean volume. Listen for hiss with sensitive headphones.
Calculate the drop and check the one-eighth rule Optional

For the resistive example, Vload/Vopen = Rload/(Rsource + Rload). With a 32 Ω load, 32/(10 + 32) ≈ 0.762. At 100 Hz with the varying load, the ratio is 48/(10 + 48). At 1 kHz it is 16/(10 + 16). The ratio between those two gains is about 1.345, or +2.57 dB.

Real impedance can have both magnitude and phase. Use the complex impedance of the source and the load at each frequency, then take the magnitude of the voltage ratio. Under the simple resistive model, a 1:8 source-to-load ratio gives a gain of 8/9, about −1.02 dB relative to open circuit.

A practical rule of thumb is to look for a jack output impedance no more than about one-eighth of the headphone’s minimum impedance. A 16 Ω minimum suggests about 2 Ω or less. Use the actual impedance curve and output measurements when available.
