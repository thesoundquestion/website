LESSON 12 · HEADPHONES

Why can the same headphones sound different on two sources?

A headphone jack can lose some voltage when you connect a load. If the headphone’s impedance changes with frequency, the amount lost can change across the music.

What the rating tells you

10 Ω output impedance describes how the source behaves under load at the stated 1 kHz test frequency. With 1.000 V RMS before connecting a load, the example supplies 0.500 V to a 10 Ω load and about 0.762 V to a 32 Ω load at the same setting.

The two marked points show the same source with different loads. The unloaded voltage is part of this example’s setup. The source’s maximum clean output and behaviour across frequency need their own tests.

When voltage loss changes the sound balance

Two cases use a headphone that measures 16 Ω at 1 kHz and 48 Ω at 100 Hz. The third case uses a flat 32 Ω load.

Showing: 1 Ω source
Calculated electrical change. Each trace is aligned at 1 kHz. A rise shows where less voltage was lost relative to that reference.

1 Ω source

A small change with a varying load

The load is three times higher at 100 Hz than at 1 kHz. With a 1 Ω source, that raises the 100 Hz voltage by about 0.35 dB relative to 1 kHz.

A low source impedance keeps the delivered voltage closer to the same proportion across the band.

10 Ω source

The impedance peak creates a bigger rise

The same headphone on a 10 Ω source shows about +2.57 dB at 100 Hz relative to 1 kHz. More voltage is lost where the headphone’s impedance is lower.

That could be heard as a change in bass balance. The frequency and size of the change depend on the actual impedance curve.

10 Ω, flat load

The level drops evenly

A flat 32 Ω load on the 10 Ω source gets 0.762 of the unloaded voltage at every frequency in the model. That is about 2.36 dB of broadband attenuation.

The graph reads 0 dB across the band because it shows relative balance after aligning the levels at 1 kHz.

Think of the source and load as a voltage divider

The source has an internal output impedance. The headphone has a separate input impedance. Together they determine how much signal voltage arrives at the headphone terminals. As the load impedance rises, the headphone receives a larger fraction of the available voltage.

A headphone’s nominal ohm rating is one number. Its impedance can vary across frequency because of driver resonance or the crossover in a multi-driver IEM. With enough source impedance, those variations can make the headphone’s response change.

Voltage division is one part of matching. Check sensitivity, clean voltage and current for headroom, and check source noise separately if a sensitive IEM reveals hiss.

Is the source changing the balance or just the level?

For a fair comparison, use the same headphones, EQ and recording, then match levels before comparing the tonal balance. The overall voltage drop on the flat load explains how a source can sound quieter with the same volume setting.

You might enjoy the bass lift that one pairing produces. A small output impedance helps preserve the headphone’s tuning across different sources.

COMMON CLAIM

“A higher-impedance headphone jack always sounds warmer.”

WHAT TO CHECK

The headphone’s impedance curve determines which frequencies receive relatively more or less voltage. A flat load mainly changes level in this model. A peak in the bass can create a lift there, and a peak elsewhere can shift the balance in another region.

What should you look for?

  1. Look for a measured source output impedance and, where available, a headphone impedance curve.
  2. For a quick check, look for source impedance near one-eighth of the headphone’s minimum impedance or lower. For a 16 Ω minimum, that gives about 2 Ω. Treat it as a rule of thumb for keeping response changes small.
  3. Also check clean output at your load, usable volume control and noise. These are separate parts of the match.
Engineering deep dive Optional

For the resistive example, Vload/Vopen = Rload/(Rsource + Rload). With a 32 Ω load, 32/(10 + 32) ≈ 0.762. At 100 Hz with the varying load, the ratio is 48/(10 + 48). At 1 kHz it is 16/(10 + 16). The ratio between those two gains is about 1.345, or +2.57 dB.

Real impedance can have both magnitude and phase. Use the complex impedance of the source and the load at each frequency, then take the magnitude of the voltage ratio. Under the simple resistive model, a 1:8 source-to-load ratio gives a gain of 8/9, about −1.02 dB relative to open circuit.
