LESSON 10 · HEADPHONES

Why can the same headphones lose bass?

A frequency range gives you the endpoints. The response curve shows the balance across that range, and the way the headphone sits can change the curve.

Read the fixture and the reference line

A headphone measurement rig uses an ear simulator or an artificial ear to estimate the sound pressure near the eardrum. The fixture’s shape and acoustics influence the raw curve. The raw response of a well-tuned headphone will usually have visible rises and dips.

A compensated graph shows the difference from a chosen target. Its 0 dB line means the headphone meets that target at that frequency. Check which target and fixture were used before comparing two traces. Targets can encode different bass preferences, and one model can measure differently after it’s reseated.

An IEM’s tip and insertion depth can change the response too. Tip seal often matters most in the bass. In the upper frequencies, small changes in depth and ear geometry can shift peaks and dips.

Does the change follow the headphone or the fit?

If low notes come back when you reseat the pads or adjust an IEM tip, fit is a likely factor. If both channels have a repeatable bass lift with a good seal, you may simply prefer more or less bass.

Compare with the same passage at a matched midrange level. A kick drum and bass line you know can help you hear whether the change is concentrated in the low end.

COMMON CLAIM

“They reach 20 Hz, so they must have strong sub-bass.”

WHAT TO CHECK

Look at the response level near 20 Hz and how much it changes with different fits. Measurements from the same fixture, target and seating method give a stronger basis for comparison.

What should you look for?

  1. Look for a response plot that names its fixture, reference target and normalization method.
  2. Check repeated placements or fit-consistency results, especially if you wear glasses or are comparing IEM tips.
  3. Evaluate comfort and response together. Pads, tips and seal can affect both during a normal listening session.
Engineering deep dive Optional

A compensated curve can be written as the measured level at each frequency minus the target level at that frequency, expressed in dB. If the curves are normalized at 1 kHz, a constant offset aligns them at that reference. This keeps the graph focused on tonal balance rather than absolute sensitivity.

The synthetic pad-gap case changes only the low end to make the example easy to read. A real reseating can also change the angle and distance between the driver and ear, which can alter the midrange and treble.
