LESSON 18 · DIGITAL AUDIO

Does lossless sound better than MP3?

FLAC can preserve every audio sample. MP3 and AAC make smaller files by leaving information out. But what you can hear depends on the encoding, the music, and how you compare them, not merely the number printed beside a file.

Bitrate
The number of data bits stored or transmitted each second, across the encoded stream.
Lossless
Compression that restores the same original digital audio samples when decoded.
Lossy
Compression that makes irreversible changes to reduce how much data is needed.

DECODE THE PRINTED SPECIFICATION

MP3 · 320 kb/s · stereo · CBR

320 kb/s means about 320,000 encoded bits in one second, total.

MP3 names the compression format. Stereo indicates two-channel playback. CBR means constant bitrate: the stream aims to use the same data rate throughout, rather than freely varying its average. 320 kb/s describes the combined encoded stream, not 320 kb/s for each channel.

Convert bits to bytes by dividing by eight: 320,000 ÷ 8 = 40,000 bytes per second. One minute uses about 2.40 MB, and four minutes about 9.60 MB, before small file/container overhead.

What this number doesn't tell you: which original master was used, what frequency content the encoder changed, whether an artifact is audible, or how the file will sound through your gear. A bitrate is a data-budget measurement, not a sound-quality score.

Where is FLAC on that chart?

FLAC deliberately has no fixed bar here. It is lossless compressed PCM, and its average bitrate changes with the music and encoding settings. A simple passage may compress very differently from a dense or noisy one. Assigning FLAC one universal bitrate would make a neat graph and teach the wrong lesson.

THREE WAYS TO STORE THE SAME SOURCE

PCM, FLAC and MP3 are doing different jobs.

Imagine that your original digital recording is a long page of numbers. Uncompressed PCM stores those numbers directly. FLAC stores a smarter, smaller description that can reproduce every one of those original audio numbers when decoded. MP3 or AAC reduce the data by making decisions about sound information that is less likely to be noticed.

WAV containing PCMStores original sample values, usually with little or no audio compression↓ decodeSame PCM samples
FLACCompresses the digital audio reversibly, without throwing away sample information↓ decodeSame PCM samples
MP3 / AACUses a listening-oriented model to reduce the data, intentionally discarding some information↓ decodeApproximation of original PCM

Lossless doesn't mean “better mastered.” A FLAC of a badly clipped master is still a perfectly faithful FLAC of that clipped master. Likewise, a carefully mastered MP3 can be more enjoyable than a harshly mastered FLAC of a different release.

WAV and AIFF usually carry uncompressed PCM, while .m4a is a container that might hold AAC or lossless ALAC, depending on the file. Check the actual codec, not just the filename extension. A 24-bit FLAC and a 16-bit FLAC are both lossless relative to the PCM audio they were made from, even though their underlying samples differ in precision.

Converting MP3 to FLAC does not reverse the loss. The new FLAC perfectly preserves the already-decoded MP3 result, including its existing artifacts. It does not restore the missing original information.

WHAT LOSSY CAN CHANGE

What should you actually listen for?

Lossy encoders try to remove or approximate information that the listener is unlikely to notice, using models of hearing and masking. At constrained bitrates or on difficult recordings, the approximations can become audible. They are called compression artifacts.

Pre-echo and smeared attacks

A very sharp drum or percussion hit can develop a faint “shh” just before the hit, or lose some crispness. Not every softened drum was damaged by a codec; the original recording might simply sound that way.

Swishy or warbling textures

Cymbal tails, ambience or sustained tones can sometimes sound watery, gritty or unstable as an encoder struggles with complex high-frequency material.

Less stable spatial cues

At sufficiently constrained settings, some stereo ambience or positioning detail may be simplified. That does not mean every lossy file has a narrow soundstage.

Upper-frequency changes

Some encoder settings apply low-pass filtering to spend bits where they may be more audible. A visible frequency cutoff alone does not reliably prove audible quality or reveal the original encoding history.

As bitrate and encoder quality improve, artifacts generally become harder to notice, but there is no magical bitrate where every recording becomes audibly identical for every listener. The specific encoder, version, settings, source and listener all matter.

This connects to the glossary's grainy, airy and separation descriptions. A subjective word is a clue to investigate, not proof of codec damage.

Can you reliably hear lossless versus a good MP3?

Sometimes, depending on the track, encoder, bitrate, equipment and listener. In other situations, a good lossy copy may be perceptually transparent: you cannot reliably distinguish it from the matched reference under controlled conditions. The file being smaller does not establish an audible problem.

For a fair comparison, start with the exact same master, encode the lossy copy directly from that lossless source, match playback levels, avoid visual cues, and compare blind. An ABX test asks whether an unknown “X” is A or B across repeated trials, so a claimed difference can be checked rather than merely described. This lesson doesn't include real test audio or claimed ABX results; those need their own controlled demonstrations.

POPULAR CLAIM

“A 320 kb/s MP3 contains only 23% of the sound, so it must sound 77% worse.”

WHAT WE CAN ACTUALLY SAY

The data rate is roughly 23% of CD-style PCM, but codec design is deliberately selective. Data-rate reduction is not a percentage of audible quality lost. Only suitable listening tests can establish whether differences are reliably audible.

What is CBR versus VBR? Optional detail

CBR aims for a constant encoded bitrate. VBR means variable bitrate, so the encoder can devote more or fewer bits to different parts of the recording. VBR files are often described by their overall average bitrate or by an encoder quality setting, not a guaranteed bitrate at every moment. Even two 256 kb/s files made by different encoders are not automatically equivalent in audible quality.

For uncompressed stereo PCM, 16 bits × 44,100 samples/s × 2 channels = 1,411,200 bits/s. For a 320 kb/s constant-rate stream, four minutes of audio contain about 9.60 MB of encoded data. See bit depth and sample rate for the two separate PCM terms.

Which format should you actually use?

  1. Archiving CDs or a music library? Prefer genuine lossless sources such as FLAC when storage permits. They preserve the decoded PCM and can be converted to other formats later without inherited lossy damage.
  2. Listening on limited storage or bandwidth? Well-encoded MP3, AAC and other modern lossy codecs can offer useful size savings. Choose a reasonable quality setting and verify compatibility rather than worshipping one number.
  3. Already have MP3 files? Keep the original files until you obtain genuinely lossless versions. Merely re-encoding MP3 to FLAC or a higher MP3 bitrate does not restore missing information, and repeated lossy re-encoding can compound damage.
  4. Judging an upgrade? Compare like-for-like masters under controlled levels, and distinguish compression artifacts from EQ, remastering, amplification, speaker placement and room effects.
