This is one stop along the sound journey. Find out what the numbers on amplifier and speaker listings mean for music, what might matter when buying, and which differences are simply a matter of taste. Start with everyday explanations, then go deeper when you're curious.

PART ONE · AMPLIFIERS

What do amplifier numbers tell you?

These are the kinds of claims you might encounter when shopping for an amplifier. Open any row for an explanation that starts with the music, not the mathematics.

Fictional example80 watts / channelStereo amplifier · 8 Ω rated load
Power per channel80 watts per channel into 8 Ω+

In everyday language: How much power the amplifier can send to one speaker under the conditions used for the test.

What might you notice when listening?

It can help the amplifier handle demanding parts of a song, like a sudden heavy drum hit, without running out of room to get louder cleanly.

Should this affect what you buy?

More watts can be useful, but doubling the watts does not make the music sound twice as loud. Check the speaker type and the test conditions too.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Power ratings depend on the speaker load, the pitch range tested, the amount of distortion allowed, how long the test lasts, and how many channels are working. For an ideal resistor, average power equals RMS volts times RMS amps.

Power with a more demanding speaker120 watts into 4 Ω+

In everyday language: A power rating for a 4 Ω load. At the same voltage, fewer ohms draw more current. The amp has to supply that current to maintain its output.

What might you notice when listening?

Some speakers make an amplifier work harder. If the amplifier can't keep up, loud passages may become distorted or trigger protection.

Should this affect what you buy?

Don't assume an amplifier with a big 4 Ω number can handle every difficult speaker. Follow the manufacturer's connection limits.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

By Ohm’s law, the same 4 V RMS gives 0.50 A into 8 Ω and 1.00 A into 4 Ω. Actual loudspeakers change their impedance with pitch and can introduce phase differences.

Distortion (THD)0.05% at a stated test level+

In everyday language: A number describing extra sound the amplifier adds while reproducing a test note.

What might you notice when listening?

Enough extra sound can make music rough, buzzy or less clean. A very small printed percentage on its own doesn't tell you how the amplifier sounds throughout a song.

Should this affect what you buy?

Compare only when the test is described similarly. A figure at an easy volume is not the same as one measured near the amplifier's limit.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Total harmonic distortion measures harmonics relative to a reference tone. The test frequency, output power, load, measurement bandwidth and distribution of harmonics can change its interpretation.

Background noise95 dB signal-to-noise ratio+

In everyday language: How far above the amplifier's own hiss the music signal sits during a particular test.

What might you notice when listening?

More separation can help keep quiet passages free of audible hiss, although speakers, headphones and listening distance affect whether you'd notice.

Should this affect what you buy?

A large number is usually desirable, but only compare numbers measured under similar conditions.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Signal-to-noise ratios depend on the chosen reference signal level and any frequency weighting. A-weighted and unweighted figures are not directly interchangeable.

Input sensitivity200 mV+

In everyday language: How strong the signal from a music player needs to be for the amplifier to reach its rated output under a specified setup.

What might you notice when listening?

It's mainly about how easily the amplifier works with a music source, not a sound-quality score.

Should this affect what you buy?

Don't confuse this with the strongest signal the amplifier can safely accept. Many modern sources output more than 200 mV.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Input sensitivity is the rated-output input requirement at a specified gain condition. Maximum input before overload is a separate quantity.

Frequency response20 Hz–20 kHz (±1 dB)+

In everyday language: How evenly the amplifier passes along deep notes, voices and high notes.

What might you notice when listening?

An amplifier that changes certain notes more than others could alter the music's balance. Most properly functioning modern amplifiers are designed to stay very even over the main listening range.

Should this affect what you buy?

Look at how even the output is, not just how far the advertised numbers extend.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

The tolerance describes variation relative to a reference level over the stated range. Load, output level and measurement conditions affect the result.

Damping factor100 at 8 Ω+

In everyday language: A number related to how much resistance the amplifier adds at the speaker connection.

What might you notice when listening?

It can affect the electrical interaction with a speaker, but it does not automatically mean punchier bass or better timing.

Should this affect what you buy?

A bigger damping factor is not always a meaningful audible upgrade. Real speakers and cables complicate the picture.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Typically defined as the speaker test resistance divided by the amplifier's output impedance at a specified frequency. This excludes much of the frequency-dependent behavior of a real speaker.

PART TWO · LOUDSPEAKERS

What do speaker numbers tell you?

Deep bass, how loudly they play, and how demanding they are for an amplifier. These numbers can help, but one impressive specification never tells the whole story.

Fictional example6 Ω nominalTwo-way bookshelf speaker
Frequency response55 Hz–20,000 Hz (±3 dB)+

In everyday language: A guide to which low and high notes the speaker can play at roughly similar strength.

What might you notice when listening?

The speaker may handle voices and guitar well while the very deepest bass notes start fading. That doesn't mean the bass suddenly stops at 55 Hz.

Should this affect what you buy?

A frequency range without saying how much the output varies can be misleading. Room placement also changes the sound.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

A frequency-response range is normally tied to a tolerance relative to a reference level at specified measurement positions. A ±3 dB tolerance allows variation, rather than indicating perfectly flat output.

Bass extension45 Hz at −6 dB+

In everyday language: How far the speaker reaches into the deepest notes before they become much weaker than the rest of the sound.

What might you notice when listening?

Think of the deepest notes on a bass guitar or a low movie effect fading while the song carries on at the same volume setting.

Should this affect what you buy?

A speaker that makes deep bass at a very weak level may still need a subwoofer if you want those notes to have strength.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

At a given measuring position and reference, −6 dB corresponds to approximately half the sound-pressure amplitude, not half the loudness you hear. The reference conditions must be specified.

Sensitivity87 dB at 2.83 V / 3.28 ft+

In everyday language: How strongly a speaker plays when it's fed a standard test signal.

What might you notice when listening?

A more sensitive speaker may play louder at the same amplifier setting, although the actual result also depends on the amplifier and how the test was performed.

Should this affect what you buy?

It's useful for planning how much amplifier output you need, but it does not tell you whether the speaker has better bass, clarity or sound quality.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

At 2.83 V, a 4 Ω test load draws twice the current and receives twice the watts of an 8 Ω load. In this example that is about 2 W versus 1 W. Check which input the sensitivity rating uses.

Impedance6 Ω nominal+

In everyday language: A rough label describing how much electrical work a speaker asks of an amplifier.

What might you notice when listening?

A difficult load can make some amplifiers run hotter, distort sooner or shut down at higher listening levels.

Should this affect what you buy?

Check that the amplifier supports the speaker's needs, and don't assume the number stays at 6 Ω for every note.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Real impedance changes with frequency and includes a phase angle. Minimum impedance and phase behavior can reveal demands the nominal label hides.

Recommended amplifier power20–120 watts+

In everyday language: The range of amplifier power the speaker maker suggests using.

What might you notice when listening?

A speaker doesn't constantly need that many watts to make music. A quiet listening session may use very little power.

Should this affect what you buy?

Don't treat the top number as a guarantee against damage. Very loud bass, a distorted amplifier or extreme settings can still damage a speaker.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Power recommendations may reflect thermal and mechanical constraints, sensitivity, test procedures and marketing decisions. They are not a direct maximum-clean-output graph.

Woofer and tweeter5¼-inch woofer + 1-inch dome tweeter+

In everyday language: Two parts that split the musical work: one handles lower sounds and the other handles higher sounds.

What might you notice when listening?

How the two parts blend can affect whether voices and instruments sound natural. Driver size or material alone doesn't settle that.

Should this affect what you buy?

Don't assume a bigger woofer always means deeper bass, or that a metal tweeter must sound sharp.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Cone area, excursion, cabinet loading, driver resonances, crossover and dispersion interact to determine the speaker's behavior.

Crossover point2,400 Hz+

In everyday language: The approximate place where one speaker driver hands more of the music over to the other.

What might you notice when listening?

A smooth handoff can help voices and instruments sound like one continuous sound instead of disconnected pieces.

Should this affect what you buy?

The number by itself doesn't show whether the handoff is well designed.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Electrical filter settings and acoustic crossover behavior differ. Frequency, slope, phase and the drivers' off-axis output matter.

How widely sound spreadsCoverage: 90° (example)+

In everyday language: How far the speaker is intended to spread sound to people sitting away from the centre.

What might you notice when listening?

Some speakers sound more consistent as you move to another seat, while others sound best in a narrower spot.

Should this affect what you buy?

Ask what notes and how much drop in strength the coverage number refers to. One angle doesn't describe everything.

Go deeper: the technical explanation

Off-axis response and directivity vary with frequency and are better assessed across multiple angles and acoustic measurements.

Here's the important difference: A speaker claiming to play a very deep note may produce that note only faintly. Think of it as turning down just the deepest bass, not lowering the volume of the entire song. That is why the number beside the bass range needs an explanation.
