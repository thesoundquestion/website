LESSON 02 · LOUDSPEAKERS · THE DEEP NOTES

Why can a speaker play bass but still lack real low-end weight?

A close look at bass roll-off, the difference between punch and deep extension, and how a spec can hide weak output.

YOUR EXAMPLE SPECIFICATION, EXPLAINED

Decode the example: 45 Hz at −6 dB

Illustrative diagram, not a manufacturer's measured response or verification of a product.

45 Hz identifies the bass note; −6 dB identifies how far its output has fallen.

The manufacturer is describing the speaker's relative bass output at one specified pitch. At 45 Hz, output is 6 decibels lower than the chosen reference level. The orange dot marks that single point; the blue roll-off is only one possible curve.

What it doesn't tell you: This doesn't mean the speaker stops making sound below 45 Hz, and it doesn't specify how loudly or cleanly it reproduces bass. The reference level and measurement method matter.

FIRST, THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

Frequency response figures sometimes list a lower number such as 45 Hz with a second figure such as −6 dB. That second figure tells you how much weaker the sound has become compared with a reference level.

Deep bass does not suddenly shut off at a single number. Most speakers gradually lose strength as notes get lower, and the shape of that decline matters.

The bass you feel, the punch you hear, and the lowest note a speaker can reproduce are related but different experiences.

CONNECT THE GRAPH TO THE MUSIC

What could you actually hear?

A kick drum has more than one part: a low thump and a faster attack. A speaker might reproduce the attack clearly while the low thump is reduced.

A bass guitar may remain audible even when its deepest fundamentals are weak, because higher parts of the sound are still present. That is why listening impressions alone can make extension difficult to judge.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“Tight bass,” “fast bass,” “one-note bass,” and “authoritative bass” are common descriptions. The terms can describe real differences, but they do not uniquely identify a woofer material or amplifier damping factor.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

A well-measured low-frequency curve can show where bass loses strength. Whether the bass sounds boomy or powerful in your room also depends heavily on placement, room modes, distortion and how loud the system is pushed. A −6 dB point is not the lowest note the speaker can make.

TAKE THIS TO THE SHOP

Before you decide what to buy

  1. Check the level attached to the lowest quoted frequency, not just the frequency itself.
  2. Compare low-bass strength and output limits, especially if you will not use a subwoofer.
  3. Consider room position and whether your favorite music actually contains deep bass.

What else can change the outcome?

Room boundaries, subwoofers, listening position, cabinet tuning, bass management, driver excursion, and the recorded bass content.

Want the engineering explanation?Advanced detail

A −6 dB response means one-half the measured sound-pressure amplitude compared with the stated reference, not half the perceived loudness. This illustration uses a relative level scale and log-spaced frequency axis. Real published responses may be free-field, ground-plane or in-room measurements, which are not interchangeable.
