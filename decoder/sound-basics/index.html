LESSON 01 · THE BASICS · NO AUDIO EXPERIENCE NEEDED

What are frequency, dB and distortion?

Every recording, speaker test and audio review uses these ideas. You can understand all three without doing any maths. Start with a song you know and imagine what changes between the recording and your ears.

A FICTIONAL SPEAKER SPECIFICATION 60 Hz–15 kHz (±3 dB)

We'll decode this example below. These numbers describe an invented speaker, not a measurement of a real model.

01 / PITCH

Frequency: how fast sound vibrates.

Pluck a bass string and it vibrates slowly enough to produce a low note. Strike a small bell and much faster vibration produces higher notes. We call the rate of vibration frequency and measure it in hertz (Hz), or cycles per second. Frequency tells you about pitch, not how loudly the note is played.

40 HzSlow vibrations

Deep rumble and low bass notes.

1,000 HzMiddle-range vibrations

Part of the familiar range of voices and instruments.

10,000 HzFast vibrations

High-frequency detail such as cymbal shimmer.

Human hearing is often described as roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz) for a young person with normal hearing. It is not a promise that everyone hears every frequency equally well: hearing varies with age, level and the person.

A real note is usually more than one frequency. A guitar string has a lowest repeating rate, called its fundamental, plus higher frequencies called harmonics. Those layers help a guitar and a piano playing the same note sound different.

02 / THE MUSICAL RANGE

Instruments overlap. There are no neat little boxes.

These are very rough areas to listen for, not fixed borders. Many instruments spread across several areas at once.

20–80 Hz Sub-bass

Deep kick and bass weight. Low notes you may feel as much as hear.

80–250 Hz Bass

Bass guitar body, kick weight and the lower body of some voices.

250 Hz–2 kHz Mids

Guitar notes, piano, vocals and much of a song's recognizable body.

2–6 kHz Presence

Speech clarity, guitar bite, snare attack and some cymbal detail.

6–20 kHz High treble

Cymbal shimmer, sharp edges and fine high-frequency detail.

A kick drum is not confined to one bass number: its deep thump may live low down while the beater's click reaches much higher. Guitar and voice can compete in the mids. This overlap is why one instrument can mask another, making it harder to follow.

Mixing sets the relative levels of individual instruments. EQ raises or lowers selected frequency areas: a +3 dB boost around 100 Hz can make several low instruments fuller, while a small cut around 300 Hz may help parts separate. EQ changes their balance, not which note is being played. Every change is a trade-off.

03 / RELATIVE LEVEL

Decibels: how much louder or quieter?

dB stands for decibel. It describes a difference or level compared with a stated reference. On a speaker's frequency-response graph, 0 dB is our comparison line, not silence.

−3 dB

That frequency is lower than our reference level.

0 dB

Our chosen reference line, not “no sound.”

+3 dB

That frequency is higher than our reference level.

Think of a recipe: if the bass gets +3 dB while the midrange stays at 0, the bass ingredients stand out more. The entire song does not have to be turned up. A difference of 3 dB is worth noticing, but it is not “twice as loud.” A roughly 10 dB change is sometimes described as about twice as loud to a listener, depending on the sound and circumstances.

Your ears also do not treat all pitches equally. Deep bass often needs more level than middle frequencies to seem equally loud. This is why the measurement line and the feeling of loudness are two different things.

04 / READING A SPEAKER GRAPH

Can a speaker keep the original balance?

Imagine that the recording already contains exactly the balance the artist approved. A speaker should ideally avoid making some notes too strong and others too weak. The straight 0 dB line means the speaker would give an equal output for equal-level test tones at every frequency. It does not mean a real song contains equally loud bass, midrange and treble. Left to right = pitch.

20 Hz is very deep bass. 20 kHz is very high treble. The spacing is logarithmic: every doubling of frequency gets comparable room.

Up and down = relative level.

At +3 dB, a tone is stronger than the chosen 0 dB reference. At −3 dB, it is weaker. This does not measure the loudness of the whole song.

±2 or ±3 dB = a tolerance band.

If the speaker stays within that band through the stated frequencies, it keeps those notes reasonably consistent in level. ±2 dB is a tighter band than ±3 dB.

The important catch: “60 Hz–15 kHz ±3 dB” does not promise strong output at 30 Hz or 20 kHz. It also says nothing by itself about distortion, timing, how sound spreads into the room, or response away from straight ahead. A smooth response matters, but it is not the complete original waveform.

05 / CHANGING THE SIGNAL

Distortion: when something new gets added or the shape changes.

Imagine playing a clean note through an amplifier and turning it up until the amplifier can no longer follow its shape. The sound coming out may gain extra tones or sound rougher: that change is distortion. A severe example is clipping, when the peaks flatten because the equipment runs out of clean output room (called headroom).

A 200 Hz pure test tone has one intended frequency. Heavy clipping can add energy at higher multiples such as 600 Hz and 1,000 Hz, even though those weren't in the test tone. With complex music, distortion can create additional tones and products between notes. Natural instrument harmonics are already part of the recorded source; they are not automatically a fault.

EQ and distortion are not the same.

Turning a frequency up with ordinary EQ changes the level of sound already present. Clipping and other nonlinear distortion can introduce additional frequencies that were not in the input. Musicians sometimes choose guitar distortion creatively; an amplifier or speaker adding unwanted distortion during playback is a different situation.

A frequency-response line shows relative levels across pitch. It does not, by itself, tell you how much distortion the speaker adds. You need separate measurements, and the amount added often changes with playback volume.

06 / THE EDGES OF HEARING

Can we feel what we cannot hear?

Below about 20 Hz: “I feel the bass.”

Very low frequencies can cause physical vibrations and pressure sensations, especially with a powerful subwoofer and at high levels. You might feel a seat or the room shake. That doesn't prove those frequencies are being heard as ordinary notes, nor does it make stronger sub-bass automatically more faithful. Keep levels safe.

Above about 20 kHz: “It adds air.”

People often call a sound “airy” or “present,” but those descriptions usually involve audible upper frequencies, not proof of hearing ultrasound. A published 30 kHz or 40 kHz speaker limit is not evidence of an audible benefit. Ultrasonic output can also create unwanted effects in playback equipment; the claim needs controlled tests, not a bigger number on a box.

The practical goal: Reproduce the audible parts of the recording evenly and cleanly at the levels you actually use. Your room, your ears and your taste still matter. “Technically different” and “audibly better” are not synonyms.
