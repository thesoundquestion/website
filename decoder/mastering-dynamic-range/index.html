LESSON 23 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 4

Mastering, remastering and dynamic range

You put on two editions of the same album. In one, the snare jumps out of the band. In the other, the band sounds fuller and the snare sits closer to the guitars. The players can be exactly the same. What happened between the studio mix and the copy you’re hearing?

What happens after the band is mixed?

  1. Recorded tracksSeparate takes of the voice, instruments or programmed parts.
  2. Finished mixThe parts sit together at chosen levels and positions.
  3. MasterThe approved mix is checked and prepared for release.
  4. Release and playbackThe edition reaches your system as a file, disc or record.

Mixing places the individual parts

The mix engineer can turn a guitar down, move it left, shorten its echo or soften its treble without changing the voice. Shaping tone by adjusting the balance from bass to treble is called equalization, or EQ. The producer may guide the arrangement, performance and overall feel. Try the four-track mix in Lesson 22. See how each instrument can have different compression.

Mastering works on the finished picture

The mastering engineer usually starts with the approved stereo mix. They may even out the tone across songs, preserve a big drum hit, tame a sharp sound or leave the mix alone and check it. They also set running order, gaps and fades, check for clicks or overload, and prepare the audio and delivery information for each format. Abbey Road’s mastering overview.

One song, different decisions. A mix engineer might keep a bass guitar steady, control the snare hits on a separate drum microphone, and leave more of the cymbals' rise and fade intact. Once those parts are mixed together, a typical stereo mastering pass works on their combined sound rather than turning down only the snare microphone. See the four-track waveform example.

If the voice and guitar both have a sharp edge in the same part of the treble, an EQ move on the whole mix can soften both. The mix engineer still has the separate guitar and voice tracks. Some mastering jobs use stems, such as vocals and instruments delivered as separate groups, which give the mastering engineer more control.

What the mastering engineer can adjust
  • Tone and cleanup. Broad EQ can rebalance the low end or top end. A de-esser turns down a sharp “s” only when it pokes out. Restoration tools can reduce hum, hiss or clicks on older material, sometimes at the cost of some texture.
  • Level and movement. Compression can act on the whole signal or on a frequency band, such as an unruly low end. Limiting controls the tallest peaks. Some engineers use controlled clipping for level or texture. Peak checks, fades and album sequencing help prepare the release.
  • Stereo. Processing the shared center information and the differences between left and right can alter the sense of width. That move affects any parts in those signals. The separate tracks are where an engineer can move or reshape one instrument on its own.

What are you getting when you buy a remaster?

Original recording
The performances and sounds captured in the session. The original takes can be used again decades later.
Original mix
The balance prepared for the first release, including the parts’ levels, positions and effects.
Original mastered version
The version prepared for the first release. The first LP and CD can have different preparation.
Remaster
A later preparation of an existing mix or source. It can keep the balances of the original mix while changing overall tone, peaks, level or restoration.
Remix
A new balance made from multitracks or other separated sources. A separate 5.1, Atmos or binaural mix can change where parts are placed and how they interact. See how spatial mixes work.
Reissue
A new release of an existing title. It may contain an earlier master, a remaster, a remix or several of these together.

“From the original master tape” still leaves a question. Ask which tape. It could be the finished stereo mixdown or a production tape assembled and prepared for a particular release. The transfer, processing and cutting decisions also affect the sound of the copy you buy.

Same drummer, three mastering choices

Picture a kick and snare over a bass line. The peak level tells you how big the strongest hit is. The average level takes the whole passage into account, including the bass and the drum ring between hits. The chart measures that average electrical level in volts RMS so we can work out how much power the amp delivers over the passage.

What a compressor does

It turns down the signal when it gets past a chosen level, called the threshold. The ratio sets how much smaller the rise will be. A slower attack can let the first edge of a snare through. A longer release keeps the gain down further into the ring of the hit. Raising the level after compression can bring the sound between hits forward. FabFilter’s compressor guide.

What a limiter and a clipper do

A brickwall limiter aims to keep peaks below a ceiling by turning them down quickly. A look-ahead limiter delays the signal briefly so it can start turning it down before a peak arrives. Clipping reshapes the waveform as it runs into a limit, which creates new frequencies and can sound gritty. That can happen at the edge of a digital file’s range or when an analog circuit runs out of swing. See what happens when a signal clips.

A remaster can change the tone and the punch together

Use the same fictional mix again. The original master leaves the mix’s shape alone. For a later edition, imagine the engineer wants more bass weight and a softer top end. They make a broad EQ change, then add compression and catch the tallest hits with a limiter. Playback gain stays fixed in this comparison.

  • Listen to the weight. A little extra bass and less top can sound warmer. The original can sound brighter by comparison. The actual result depends on how much sound was in those bands to begin with.
  • Listen to the edges. A vocal can feel more forward if its strongest frequencies or quieter syllables gain prominence. Less treble can soften some sibilance, the sharp “s” and “sh” sounds. Changing treble can also change how easily you hear cymbals and breath.
  • Listen to the space around parts. EQ and compression can change how easy it is to hear reverb tails or separate a bass note from a kick. The mix still provides the positions that create imaging and the depth cues that can make a soundstage feel holographic.

Before judging how punchy the later master is, turn it down until its body sounds about as loud as the first. Play the same few seconds again. Can you still pick out the drum’s first hit and hear its ring fade?

One approved mix can take two paths to your system

The engineer may start both formats from the same prepared master. They can also go back to the mix and make separate decisions for a file or a record. The last steps change because the two formats carry sound differently.

Why a phono stage matters. Standard RIAA cutting EQ reduces bass and raises treble. The phono stage boosts the bass and reduces the treble by complementary amounts. This restores the intended balance and lowers high-frequency surface noise. The pressing, stylus, cartridge and phono stage can still affect tone, noise and the shape of a recorded peak. See the RIAA curves. SST’s lacquer-cutting notes.

How getting louder changed the sound

When listeners switched between CDs or tracks at the same volume setting, the one with more level could grab attention first. Once the tallest digital peaks were near the ceiling, engineers could get more level by reducing the peaks and raising everything together. In some releases this left less room for a snare to jump out or for notes to fade between hits. Researchers found changes to peak-to-average relationships in mainstream music during the loudness war. Larger-scale contrast, such as a quiet verse leading into a loud chorus, is a separate part of the picture. AES research on mainstream dynamics.

Some listeners like a dense, immediate wall of guitars. Others hear congestion or a harsh edge when sustained parts compete with the hits. That may get tiring for them. A recording can feel dynamic when the arrangement opens up, or have strong separation because the mix makes space for each instrument. A single peak number leaves both of those experiences unexplained.

Which kind of “loud” did the meter measure?

dBFS and sample peak
dBFS measures a digital level relative to full scale. A sample-peak meter reports the largest stored sample. This tells you how close the file’s samples are to the digital limit. Your volume setting, amplifier, speakers and room determine the sound level at your chair.
True peak (dBTP)
When a player turns the stored samples back into a continuous signal, its highest point can fall between two of those samples. A true-peak meter estimates that highest point. It helps an engineer check how much room is left for the peaks when preparing a file.
Average level (RMS)
RMS voltage tells you what steady voltage would deliver the same average power into the same load. For example, a musical passage measuring 2 V RMS delivers the same average power as a steady 2 V into the lesson’s 8 Ω load. To compare how loud two songs sound, use your ears or a loudness reading.
Overall loudness (integrated LUFS)
Estimates how loud a whole song sounds. It accounts for how sensitive our ears are to different frequencies and excludes very quiet stretches. Streaming players can use it to adjust the playback volume across tracks. A song with a huge drum hit and one with a steady backing can get similar readings.
Loudness range (LRA)
Compares the typical quiet and loud stretches, leaving out the extremes. For the size of a single drum hit, check its peak or look closely at the waveform.
Peak above average (crest factor)
The gap in dB between the peak and the average level (RMS) over the same passage. A larger gap means the peak sticks out more. Change the passage you measure and the number may change.
Musical dynamics
What you hear when a player accents a note, a drum decays or a chorus opens up. A song can keep its verse-to-chorus contrast while losing some drum attack. PCM bit depth affects how finely a file can represent amplitude and its noise floor.

Standards define how to take these measurements consistently. ITU-R BS.1770 (loudness and true peak). EBU Tech 3342 (loudness range).

What the streaming service changes at playback

Imagine a hot remaster followed by a quieter original in a playlist. With normalization on, the player may turn the remaster down and raise the original if there’s room for its peaks. A gain change moves the whole waveform together. The space between the snare and the backing survives that move, including any change to it already made in the studio.

Spotify

Spotify documents −14 LUFS in Normal mode on supported clients, plus Premium Quiet at −19 and Loud at −11. It normally uses a shared adjustment for an album played in order and track-by-track adjustments for playlists or shuffle. Available peak room can limit how far Normal raises a quiet track. Loud mode can apply a limiter during playback. Spotify says normalization is skipped on its web player and third-party devices such as some TVs and speakers. Spotify’s artist documentation.

Apple Music

Apple’s Sound Check adjusts playback level across songs. You can switch it on or off in Music’s Playback settings on a Mac or in Music settings on an iPhone or iPad. Confirm which mix you’re playing as well, since switching between stereo and Atmos can change the music’s balance and space. Apple’s Sound Check guide.

Spotify’s Normal setting is a playback reference and its artist guidance is a recommendation. A separate broadcast recommendation, EBU R 128, calls for −23 LUFS programme loudness in its own context. Mastering, player gain, playback limiting and lossy encoding can each affect a different part of what arrives at the DAC. EBU R 128.

What changes for the amp and speakers?

One version can ask for more output over time. Another can ask for a bigger burst for a fraction of a second. Both can be true of the same two releases depending on which level you hold equal. Here are two small examples, each across one ideal 8 Ω resistance.

Same peak · The denser signal uses more average power

Both reach 10 V at the peak. Between peaks, the denser signal stays at a higher level.

Average power at equal peak voltage
SignalAverage levelAverage power
More space2 V0.5 W
Denser4 V2.0 W

Four times the average power. If that higher average holds over sustained playback, the amp delivers more energy per second and the speaker’s voice coil can run warmer.

Same average · The bigger accent needs more swing

Both have the same average level, 2 V RMS, and use 0.5 W on average. One has a bigger drum hit.

Power at the loudest instant with the same average level
SignalPeakAt that instant
Taller hit10 V12.5 W
Shorter hit5 V3.125 W

Twice the peak voltage and current. That’s four times the instantaneous power. An amp needs enough headroom to make that short swing cleanly.

Show the calculations

For a fixed resistance, average power = RMS voltage² ÷ resistance. So 2² ÷ 8 = 0.5 W and 4² ÷ 8 = 2 W. At an instant, power = instantaneous voltage² ÷ resistance. So 10² ÷ 8 = 12.5 W and 5² ÷ 8 = 3.125 W. At those two peaks the currents are 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25 A and 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625 A. Crest factor = 20 × log₁₀(peak voltage ÷ RMS voltage).

Read the amp rating under its test conditions. Power at the peak tells you what happens at one instant. A continuous power rating comes from a sustained test, usually with a steady tone. A burst rating comes from a short test. A real speaker’s electrical load changes with frequency, so it can draw different amounts of current on different notes at the same voltage. Strong bass can make the cone move farther. High average power over time can warm the coil that moves it.

More clean headroom can preserve a peak that the amp would otherwise clip. If the release itself has already flattened the peak, a better amp or DAC still receives that version of the waveform. Your system’s response, distortion, DSP and room can change how prominent the effect sounds.

Could your system play those peaks cleanly?

Use your speaker sensitivity, listening distance, target levels and amp ratings to estimate the power your setup needs. Keep your average and peak goals separate.
