LESSON 18 · SPEAKERS

Why does a speaker sound different across the couch?

Move away from the middle seat and the sound can change even though the volume knob hasn't moved. Speakers send different amounts of sound in different directions, and the difference can change with pitch.

On axis (0°)
Listening straight ahead from the speaker's stated reference direction, usually near its tweeter or acoustic axis.
Off axis
Listening at an angle away from that forward direction, either sideways or above and below.
Directivity
How strongly a speaker concentrates sound in certain directions instead of spreading it everywhere.

DECODE THE EXACT STAT

What does 30° horizontal, −6 dB at 8 kHz mean?

In this example, 8 kHz sound is 6 dB lower at 30° sideways than directly in front.

Imagine measuring one speaker at the same distance in two directions while feeding it the same test signal. Straight in front is called 0°. Move the measurement microphone 30° sideways, and in this made-up example the 8 kHz sound pressure level drops by 6 dB.

A 6 dB drop in sound pressure level is about half the pressure amplitude. That's what the two bars show. It is not a claim that music suddenly sounds half as loud; perceived loudness is more complicated.

What this number cannot tell you: what happens at 1 kHz, whether the speaker is balanced on axis, how the vertical response behaves, how the room reflects sound, or how wide the best listening area is overall.

WHAT YOUR ROOM ADDS

The sound comes straight at you, and it also bounces around.

The first sound reaching you from a speaker is the direct sound. Some sound travels toward side walls, the floor and the ceiling and reaches you after a reflection. Those reflected sounds still carry the speaker's off-axis tonal balance.

A speaker that changes smoothly as you move off axis can give nearby seats and reflections a more consistent tonal character. If there is a sudden extra dip around its crossover frequency at certain angles, the direct and reflected sound may have noticeably different frequency balances. The room and placement determine how much of that difference reaches your ears.

Wide, smooth radiation

More sound reaches seats and side walls at greater angles. It may be useful for a broad listening area, but also sends more energy toward reflective surfaces.

Narrow, smooth radiation

Less off-axis energy at some frequencies can reduce side-wall contribution. It may demand more careful speaker aiming and seat positioning.

Uneven radiation

A sudden mismatch between drivers can produce response dips or peaks at some angles even if the main on-axis curve looks good.

Wider is not automatically better. Think about your actual seating arrangement, toe-in, wall distances, room treatment and how consistent the response remains as the angle changes. Different recordings and personal preferences also matter.

What are “listening window,” “sound power” and “spinorama”? Optional detail

A formal off-axis test can measure many angles in both horizontal and vertical planes. ANSI/CTA-2034, commonly called a spinorama, groups these measurements into summaries. On-axis is one reference direction. The listening window is an average of nearby response directions, including angles out to ±30° horizontally and ±10° vertically. Early reflections estimates first-bounce reflected energy using a defined angle-weighted procedure. Sound power estimates radiated acoustic output averaged over directions.

Directivity index compares the level of a forward/listening-window response with a wider-angle response. Its trend can tell you how radiation becomes more focused with frequency. A smooth trend is often more informative than a single advertised “dispersion angle.” These are standardized measurement summaries, not a guarantee of a particular room's exact response.

ANOTHER DIRECTION TO THINK ABOUT

Sideways isn't the same as above or below.

A published horizontal plot tells you what happens when you move left or right. A vertical plot describes moving above or below the speaker's reference axis, such as standing up from the sofa or sitting lower than the tweeter.

Many speakers use separate woofers and tweeters. Around the frequency where both drivers play, their sound can reinforce or cancel differently depending on your listening height. This is called vertical lobing, and it varies by speaker layout and crossover design.

Practical example:

A speaker may have smooth horizontal coverage across your couch while exhibiting a narrow vertical sweet spot near the crossover. Another design may behave differently. A horizontal “±30°” figure says nothing reliable about its vertical coverage.

When reviewing speaker measurements, look for both directions, especially if your speakers sit high, low, on a shelf, or cover people at different seated and standing heights.

Can dispersion change imaging and separation?

Yes, it can influence the direct-to-reflected balance and how the stereo image behaves as you move, but dispersion cannot create spatial information that isn't in the recording. Speaker spacing, left-right symmetry, toe-in and room reflections remain important.

COMMON CLAIM

“Wide-dispersion speakers are always more holographic and have better separation.”

WHAT THE EVIDENCE CAN SUPPORT

Off-axis measurements can describe tonal consistency and where sound is radiated. “Holographic” and “separation” describe listening impressions. Room layout and recording matter, so the claim cannot be established from a single dispersion angle.

Our listening glossary has plain-language entries for soundstage, imaging, separation and holographic. Those describe experiences; this lesson examines one possible physical contributor.

What should you check when choosing or positioning speakers?

  1. Look for on-axis and off-axis frequency response. A single “frequency range” or “120° coverage” label is far less informative than curves across multiple angles.
  2. Think about seats. For a wide couch, check response around the angles your seats make relative to each speaker, rather than assuming wider published coverage is always an advantage.
  3. Check horizontal and vertical behavior separately. Aim the acoustic reference axis at a sensible listening height and look for troublesome crossover-region dips away from it.
  4. Try positioning before replacing equipment. Small adjustments to speaker height, spacing, toe-in and distance from reflective walls can change the direct and reflected balance. Keep left/right positioning reasonably symmetrical.
  5. Compare measurements from known methods. CTA-2034-style data, directivity maps and measured listening windows can reveal much more than a marketing adjective.
How do I interpret a single “−6 dB beamwidth” number? Optional detail

For a named frequency and measurement plane, a −6 dB coverage or beamwidth can mean the angular width between directions where sound pressure has dropped 6 dB relative to the relevant reference or maximum. Check whether the published value means full included beamwidth, or an angle from the centre line, and whether it refers to horizontal or vertical coverage. The example here specifies one offset angle: 30° horizontally. It does not assert that the full beamwidth is 30° or 60°.
