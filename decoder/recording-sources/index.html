LESSON 20 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 1

Where does a recording's sound really begin?

Before the DAC, amplifier or loudspeaker gets a vote, someone has to capture or create the sounds. Today that may involve a microphone in a room, a guitar plugged straight into an interface, a keyboard synthesizer, or a computer generating the instrument.

The useful distinction: A microphone captures sound pressure; a DI captures an instrument's electrical signal; a software instrument may create audio without recording a new acoustic event at all. All three can produce outstanding recordings, or disappointing ones.

02 / MICROPHONE CHOICE

What kind of microphone, and which way is it listening?

Moving-coil dynamic

Common for vocals, loud stages and close instruments. Output level, response and suitability depend on the model. Often robust, but not automatically less detailed.

Condenser

Uses an electrically biased capsule and active electronics. Often chosen for nuanced sources and lower self-noise, but overload, pattern and frequency response still depend on design.

Ribbon

A thin conductive ribbon moves in a magnetic field, a type of dynamic transducer. Often bidirectional in classic designs. Some are delicate or need extra gain; modern models vary.

Manufacturers offer different responses, polar patterns and maximum sound-pressure capabilities. Price and microphone category alone cannot tell you what a voice will sound like.

Cardioid

Favours sound in front and reduces pickup at the rear in an idealized pattern. At very close range, many directional mics show a proximity-effect bass increase.

Omnidirectional

A pressure-operated ideal omni responds similarly from every horizontal direction. It does not exhibit the directional pressure-gradient proximity effect, but distance still changes the direct-to-room balance.

Figure-eight

Picks up front and back with deep nulls to either side in the ideal model. Bidirectional ribbon and some condenser configurations can show strong proximity effect.

A polar diagram shows direction sensitivity, not a mic's complete tonal response. Real patterns change with frequency, and off-axis sound may be coloured. Real-world responses are rarely mathematically perfect hearts, circles or figure-eights.

03 / MOVE THE MIC

The room and placement become part of the recording.

Keep the singer and fictional 12 × 15 ft room the same. Change microphone distance and imagine how the balance of direct sound and room reflections might shift. This is a placement demonstration, not a simulation of measured room acoustics.

Close: 0.5 ft

More direct sound relative to the room; often better isolation. A directional mic may add proximity-effect bass.

A singer six inches from a directional mic can have pronounced plosives or bass lift. A pop filter and consistent positioning can help.

Moderate: 2 ft

A different balance of direct sound and room reflections; not automatically the most natural choice.

Small changes in aim may change voice tone. Reflections, mic pattern and the room's surfaces matter.

Farther: 6 ft

A smaller direct-to-room ratio may make ambience and other instruments more apparent.

A farther mic may capture welcome spaciousness in a good room or excessive reflections in a poor one.

Closer often means more direct sound.

The direct signal typically falls with distance. As the mic moves farther away, reflected sound can become more important. In an ideal free field, doubling distance reduces direct sound pressure by about 6 dB; a real room adds reflections and boundaries, so that rule does not predict a precise in-room recording level.

Mic angle and bass matter too.

Moving a directional microphone closer may exaggerate bass through proximity effect. Turning a microphone slightly off-axis can reduce plosives or change brightness, depending on the mic. An ideal pressure omni does not have the same proximity-effect mechanism.

04 / WHAT YOU ACTUALLY HEAR

Connect capture decisions to the listening glossary.

Warm / bright

Mic angle, proximity, source radiation and amp-cabinet placement can change tonal balance before a mixer adds EQ.

Soundstage / imaging

Stereo microphone technique and captured ambience can establish directional and distance cues. The mix and playback room can change how those cues appear.

Separation / congestion

Mic bleed, overlapping instrument ranges, arrangement and time alignment all influence what later mix processing can separate.

Dynamic / punchy

Performance, drum capture and transient shape matter before compressors or mastering limiters reshape them. Capture alone does not dictate a finished song's dynamics.

A playback system can reproduce or colour the information it receives, but it cannot restore microphone detail that was never captured or undo an irreversibly flattened mix. DSP can create new spatial effects, though those are not missing original information magically recovered.

05 / ANALOGUE VERSUS DIGITAL

Why doesn't a modern recording automatically sound better?

A carefully performed live band can sound natural and engaging on tape or in a modern digital studio. A virtual-instrument production can be equally inventive and dynamic. What differs is what the engineer and artist can choose: capture a shared room, isolate a DI track, quantize drums, replace a snare sound, automate effects or build a convincingly artificial space.

What digital tools make easier

  • Clean capture, consistent edits and non-destructive changes.
  • Re-amping, layered sampled instruments and precise automation.
  • Mixing music with no newly miked instrument, or blending live recordings with software.
  • Reproducible mixes and immersive spatial production.

Where production can still disappoint

  • Excessive editing or quantization can change the feel of a performance.
  • Poorly chosen samples, cabinet simulations or room effects can sound unnatural.
  • Heavy compression and limiting can reduce peak-to-average contrast.
  • A poor room or microphone position can capture problems that EQ cannot fully undo.

None of those traits belongs exclusively to one era. Comparing an older release with a remaster of the same album is particularly tricky because the **mastering itself** may be different.
