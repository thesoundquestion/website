LESSON 13 · SOURCES & DACS

Why doesn't a stronger source automatically distort an amplifier?

Your player might send 2 volts while the amp says it needs only 200 millivolts. That sounds like a mismatch. The numbers answer different questions.

Source output level
The strength of the electrical signal leaving a music player.
Input sensitivity
How much signal the amplifier needs to reach rated output under a stated setup. It is NOT the strongest signal it can accept.
Maximum clean input
The highest signal the input stage can handle within a defined distortion limit.

THE EXACT PRINTED SPECIFICATION

What do these numbers actually mean?

2 V from the player versus 0.20 V needed for rated amplifier output.

2.0 V RMS at 0 dBFS describes a source's output during a full-scale digital sine-wave test. 200 mV RMS input sensitivity says 0.20 V is enough for rated amplifier output under the specified test and gain settings.

The source test level is ten times the sensitivity voltage, a 20 dB voltage-level difference. That does not mean the input will distort. A volume control can attenuate a signal, but its location in the circuitry matters.

Not stated: the input's maximum clean signal, the amplifier's early gain-stage arrangement, or whether actual programme material reaches the source's full-scale voltage.

Unknown is the correct answer

The 200 mV sensitivity figure does not provide a maximum input rating. You cannot use it to prove or dismiss overload.

Understand the result

The source sets an analogue signal level. The amplifier's input sensitivity describes how much input signal is needed to get a certain rated output. The maximum clean input is the limit beyond which the input stage may add too much distortion.

These are different numbers. A large source voltage may simply mean less volume-control gain or more attenuation is needed. A lower source volume only helps with input distortion if the distorted stage actually receives a smaller signal.

Even the common −10 dBV consumer and +4 dBu professional audio references (about 0.316 and 1.23 V RMS, respectively) are nominal conventions, not universal maximum voltages. An RCA connection does not automatically mean a fixed 0.316 V output.

Calculate the 20 dB difference Optional maths

20 × log₁₀(2 / 0.2) = 20 dB. This compares the printed voltage values. It is not a statement that listening will be 20 dB louder after adjusting the volume knob. For the effect of voltage on current and power at different loads, use our simple Ohm's law example.

What could you notice when listening?

If an amplifier handles the source cleanly, lowering a computer or DAC output may only make you turn the amplifier up further. If the incoming voltage exceeds a real early input stage's clean range, lowering the signal before that stage can improve the result.

Distortion that appears mainly at high speaker playback levels may instead involve the amplifier output stage, its power supply or the speaker itself. See power and headroom rather than assuming the source is responsible.

POPULAR SHORTCUT

“200 mV input sensitivity means anything above 200 mV will distort.”

WHAT THE STAT SUPPORTS

It establishes a rated-output requirement, not the overload point. A missing maximum input rating means the overload margin is unknown.

What should you check?

  1. Check that your source's line output is connected to a line input, not a phono or microphone input.
  2. Distinguish the source's nominal level from its full-scale or maximum clean output.
  3. Find the next device's maximum clean input when available; don't substitute the sensitivity figure.
  4. Compare settings at matched listening levels and avoid risky loud tests.
