LESSON 17 · SOURCES & DACS

What does 16-bit or 24-bit audio actually change?

Imagine taking little snapshots of a moving sound wave. Each snapshot needs a number to describe how high or low the wave is at that moment. Bit depth tells us how many different numbers we can choose from. More choices mean less rounding, not extra instruments, new notes or an automatically better song.

START WITH ONE IDEA

More bits give the recorder more level choices.

Few available levels

A sound's level may fall between the choices, so the recorder must round.

Many available levels

With more choices, the saved number can sit closer to the original level.

Picture a measuring ruler. A ruler marked only at whole inches forces you to round. A ruler with smaller marks lets you record a more precise length. Bit depth works similarly, but for the height of an audio signal.

With 16 bits, a digital audio sample can use 65,536 different values. With 24 bits, it can use 16,777,216. That's 256 times as many possible values, giving 256 times finer level spacing on a comparable scale.

It doesn't mean 24-bit music has 256 times more detail you can hear. The original microphone, recording noise, mastering choices and your playback system still matter.

What do “PCM,” “signed” and “quantization” mean? Optional vocabulary

PCM (pulse-code modulation) means storing sound as a series of numbers. Linear means equal numerical steps represent equal changes in signal level. Signed means the numbers can be positive or negative, matching a wave that moves above and below its centre line. Quantization is simply the rounding to the nearest number that can be stored.

In signed 16-bit PCM, the stored codes run from −32,768 to +32,767. Their binary representation uses sixteen 0-or-1 positions. This is how the 65,536 possible patterns arise, not 65,536 musical notes.

THE BIG MISUNDERSTANDING

More available levels don't make the band more dramatic.

Bit depth describes the recording format's capacity. Musical dynamics describe how much the song changes between quiet and loud. A drummer leaving space before a heavy crash creates musical impact. A file marked “24-bit” does not create that impact if the recording or mastering already flattened it.

16-bit · expressive master

ONE USEFUL PRODUCTION TRICK

Why would an engineer add a tiny amount of noise?

At very quiet levels, repeatedly rounding the same sound can produce an unwanted pattern. Engineers call this quantization error. The surprising solution is a tiny amount of random noise, called dither, added before reducing a finished recording to fewer bits.

Without suitable dither

Very quiet sound → repeated rounding → the error can follow the sound and become rough or tone-like.

With suitable dither

Very quiet sound → tiny random noise → the error behaves more like gentle background noise. Delicate fades can be represented more faithfully.

You do not need to add dither every time you listen. It is mainly a final-production step when changing to a lower integer bit depth, such as preparing a 16-bit CD master. It doesn't create extra bits or rescue a clipped recording.

Where do the familiar 96 dB and 144 dB figures come from? Optional maths

An ideal digital system gains roughly 6 dB of possible range for each extra bit. So 16 bits is roughly 96 dB and 24 bits roughly 144 dB by that shortcut. A more specific textbook calculation for a full-scale sine wave with ideal uniform rounding error and no dither gives 6.02 × bits + 1.76 dB, or about 98.1 and 146.2 dB.

Those are not measurements of a real DAC or how dynamic a song is. Analogue noise, recording conditions, dither and measurement bandwidth all matter. Read how DAC measurements are reported.

Can you actually hear 16-bit versus 24-bit music?

Often, the bigger audible difference comes from the recording and mastering, not the number on the file. A well-made, properly dithered 16-bit version can already reproduce music extremely well under typical listening conditions.

A 24-bit recording is valuable while you work: it gives you far more space for low-level information when leaving extra headroom for unexpected peaks. But a released 24-bit file does not automatically have wider soundstage, clearer instruments or more powerful drums than its 16-bit counterpart.

COMMON CLAIM

“24-bit always means more detail, deeper soundstage and bigger dynamics.”

WHAT THE SPEC ACTUALLY SUPPORTS

More possible numerical levels for each snapshot. The mix, mastering, source noise and playback chain determine whether that extra precision makes an audible difference.

Which bit depth should you choose?

  1. Listening to music? A well-mastered 16-bit recording is already excellent. Choose the master you prefer rather than automatically paying more for a 24-bit badge.
  2. Recording a voice, instrument or band? Start with 24-bit recording, typically at 44.1 or 48 kHz. You can leave room for loud unexpected notes without pushing the recording close to the top of the meter.
  3. Making a finished 16-bit file? Use suitable dither when reducing the integer bit depth, normally at the final export stage.
  4. Buying a DAC? Support for 24-bit files isn't the same as achieving 24 bits of noise-free analogue performance. Look at actual measurements and practical listening conditions.
Does 24-bit take more storage space? Optional detail

At the same sample rate and channel count, 24-bit uncompressed PCM uses 50% more audio data than 16-bit. For stereo 44.1 kHz audio, the raw rates are 1,411.2 kilobits/s and 2,116.8 kilobits/s. Formats such as FLAC compress losslessly, so their actual file sizes vary.
