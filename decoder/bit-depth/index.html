LESSON 16 · SOURCES & DACS

Is 24-bit music better than 16-bit?

Both can sound excellent. Bit depth mainly controls how finely each sound sample's level can be represented. It does not turn up the music, add instruments, or guarantee more musical drama.

Bit depth
How many binary digits are available for the level of each sample.
Quantization
Rounding a level to the nearest allowed digital value.
Dynamic range
The separation between loud and quiet levels, but the phrase can describe different things in music and equipment.

DECODE THE PRINTED STAT

What does “16-bit signed PCM” actually mean?

16 bits give 65,536 distinct numbers for each audio sample.

Every bit can be a 0 or 1. Using 16 of them gives 2¹⁶ = 65,536 possible patterns. Signed 16-bit linear PCM maps these patterns to sample codes from −32,768 to +32,767.

At each sample instant, the audio signal's level is rounded to one of these numbers. Zoom very close to silence and you can see the tiny steps. One least significant bit (LSB) is the size of a single step in the numerical sample code.

Important: this is amplitude precision, not the number of samples taken each second. That separate number is sample rate. A real DAC does not reconstruct sound by connecting a jagged stair-step waveform.

Original mathematical illustration: exactly 16 binary positions and six fictional signal samples quantized to signed PCM codes near zero. The lower graph is enormously magnified; the full digital range contains 65,536 codes.

How small is one step?

On a common normalized signed-PCM scale, the difference between adjacent 16-bit codes is 1/32,768 of full-scale peak amplitude. A 24-bit step is 256 times smaller on that same scale. The total possible range is not a claim about how much clean detail any particular microphone or DAC actually captures.

The well-known shortcut is about 6 dB per additional bit. The quoted 98.1 and 146.2 dB values use an ideal full-scale sine wave, uniform quantization-error model, and no added dither. Dither, noise shaping, output level, ADC/DAC limitations and measurement bandwidth change real results.

TWO DIFFERENT MEANINGS OF DYNAMICS

More digital range is not the same as a more dynamic recording.

Imagine recording a whisper followed by a drum strike. Bit depth defines how many numeric amplitude levels the format makes available between its limits. Musical dynamics depend on the performance, mixing, mastering and level differences in the finished recording. A heavily compressed 24-bit master can be less dynamic than an open, carefully mastered 16-bit track.

Digital-format precision

A 24-bit file offers many more possible amplitude values than a 16-bit file. That makes it easier to keep headroom and very quiet material during recording or processing.

Musical drama

A band going from a soft verse to a powerful chorus is an arrangement and production choice. Bigger numbers on a file do not recreate dynamics removed during mastering.

When recording, 24-bit working files are useful because engineers can leave generous room for unpredictable peaks without pushing quiet sounds close to the digital quantization floor. Working software often also uses floating-point processing for different reasons; floating-point internal precision is not the same specification as 24-bit integer PCM.

At playback, 16-bit delivery can already be extremely quiet compared with typical listening environments when the recording is competently dithered and the chain is clean. A speaker, room or headphone's actual noise and distortion may matter far more than the extra digital codes.

Why do some guides say 96 dB, while our chart shows 98.1 dB? Optional maths

The rough rule is 6 dB per bit, giving approximately 96 dB for 16 bits and 144 dB for 24 bits. The ideal quantization signal-to-noise calculation for a full-scale sine wave is 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB, or about 98.1 and 146.2 dB for 16 and 24 bits. The extra 1.76 dB comes from the chosen full-scale sine-wave reference and uniform-error assumptions; it isn't a secret bonus in every recording.

These theoretical numbers are not interchangeable with a measured DAC's signal-to-noise or dynamic-range rating. A converter can handle a 24-bit digital stream without resolving all 24 bits above its analogue electronics' noise.

THE COUNTERINTUITIVE PART

Why would anyone add noise to improve digital audio?

When music gets extremely quiet, rounding each sample to a smaller set of numbers can create repeating errors related to the signal. Those errors may sound like unwanted tones or roughness rather than a harmless background hiss.

Dither is deliberately adding a very small, random noise signal before reducing the audio to a lower integer bit depth. It makes the rounding errors less correlated with the music, trading some very low-level noise for reduced quantization distortion and more faithful statistical representation of delicate signals and fade-outs.

Without suitable ditherVery quiet signal + rounding → errors can follow the music
With suitable ditherVery quiet signal + tiny noise + rounding → mostly noise-like error

This does not create extra bits or recover lost recording information. Noise-shaped dither can move more of that noise into frequencies where it is generally less noticeable, with trade-offs. It belongs mainly in a controlled production/export workflow, especially when converting a finished master to 16-bit integer PCM, not as a mysterious “enhance music” toggle on your stereo.

There is no single dither noise-floor figure that applies to every algorithm. That is why the interactive comparison above uses a clearly labeled ideal non-dithered quantization calculation rather than pretending all real 16-bit files have precisely the same noise level.

When should 24-bit playback sound different?

A real difference can happen if you compare files with different masters, processing, levels or conversion quality, or if the system has genuine limitations. Those differences should not automatically be credited to bit depth itself. If a properly dithered 16-bit version and a 24-bit version come from the same master, any difference can be extremely difficult to demonstrate under ordinary playback conditions.

COMMON CLAIM

“24-bit files have greater depth, holographic imaging and more powerful musical dynamics.”

WHAT THE SPEC SUPPORTS

24-bit PCM supplies finer digital amplitude precision and a lower ideal quantization floor. It does not establish a better master, different spatial cues or a larger loud-to-quiet contrast in a finished song.

The listening glossary distinguishes dynamic, detailed and holographic impressions from their possible measurable causes.

Which bit depth should you choose?

  1. Playing well-mastered music? Good 16-bit delivery is already a high-quality standard. Don't treat a 24-bit badge as an automatic upgrade.
  2. Recording or mixing music? Use 24-bit capture or a suitable floating-point workflow. The extra working range makes setting levels easier and reduces quantization constraints.
  3. Exporting to 16-bit? Apply appropriate dither when reducing integer word length as part of final delivery, avoiding unnecessary repeated reductions.
  4. Shopping for a DAC? Look at real output noise, distortion and other test conditions rather than mistaking 24-bit compatibility for 24 bits of actual analogue resolution.
What happens to uncompressed file size? Optional calculation

For uncompressed stereo PCM at 44.1 kHz: 16 bits × 44,100 samples/s × 2 channels = 1,411.2 kilobits per second. At 24 bits with the same sample rate and channels, it is 2,116.8 kilobits per second. Those are the raw PCM data rates before container overhead; compressed lossless formats such as FLAC need not have fixed bit rates. Sample rate affects the calculation separately.
