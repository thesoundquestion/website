01 / AUDIO VOCABULARY

Electricity & speaker matching

Voltage

The electrical push that makes current flow. Think of the pressure supplied by a water pump.

Picture this: More pressure can push more water through the same tap. Voltage is the electrical push an amplifier provides.

Explore the full lesson →

Current (amps)

How much electric charge flows through a circuit each second. We measure this in amperes, usually called amps.

Picture this: Like gallons per minute from a pump. A lower-impedance speaker can demand more amps, just as a wider tap asks for more water flow.

Explore the full lesson →

Resistance

How much something restricts the flow of electricity. Think of a narrower tap that restricts water.

Picture this: With the same voltage (push), higher resistance means less current (flow). To keep the same current through more resistance, you would need more voltage.

Explore the full lesson →

Impedance

How much a speaker or headphone opposes the changing electrical signal from an amplifier. A real speaker’s impedance changes with the notes it plays.

Picture this: At the same voltage, a lower-impedance speaker asks for more current. That can push an amplifier closer to its limits.

Explore the full lesson →

Ohm (Ω)

The unit used to describe resistance and impedance.

Picture this: Four and eight ohms name electrical loads, not sound quality.

Explore the full lesson →

Load

The device or circuit an amplifier must drive.

Picture this: A speaker is the amplifier’s electrical load.

Explore the full lesson →

Watts (power)

The rate at which electrical energy is delivered. In our pump example, both water pressure and flow matter when we talk about the work a pump does.

Picture this: Two speakers receiving the same watts do not necessarily play equally loud. Their sensitivity matters too.

Explore the full lesson →

RMS

A way to describe the effective strength of a changing voltage or current.

Picture this: An audio signal swings up and down; RMS helps compare its electrical strength.

Explore the full lesson →

Peak

The highest instant of a signal during a stated time.

Picture this: A drum hit can be much stronger than the average music level.

Explore the full lesson →

Current limit

A point beyond which an amplifier cannot keep supplying requested current.

Picture this: A pump cannot deliver unlimited water just because a tap opens wider.

Explore the full lesson →

Speaker sensitivity

How much sound a speaker makes for a defined test input and distance.

Picture this: Two speakers given the same electrical power can be different loudness levels.

Explore the full lesson →

Output impedance

Impedance presented by the source or amplifier output.

Picture this: A headphone jack can interact with a headphone’s changing impedance.

ELECTRICITY WITHOUT THE EQUATIONS

One pump. Two taps. Different demands.

Picture the amplifier as a water pump. The water pressure is the voltage, and the water flowing is the current. At the same pressure, a wide bathtub tap needs more water than a narrow sink tap. But opening a bigger tap doesn't make the pump stronger. If a speaker asks for more electrical current than the amplifier can safely supply, the sound may become rough or distorted. The amplifier might reduce its output or shut off to protect itself, much like a breaker tripping. If it is pushed beyond its limits for too long, parts can overheat, wear out sooner or fail. Very distorted or excessive output can damage the speakers, too. A 4-ohm speaker isn't automatically dangerous: what matters is whether the amplifier can handle that particular speaker at the volume you use.

Amplifiers & levels

Gain

How much an audio circuit boosts the signal going through it. Gain is related to the familiar volume knob, but they are not necessarily the same control.

Picture this: On many home amplifiers, the volume knob changes how much signal reaches an amplifier whose gain stays fixed. A microphone preamp may have its own gain knob that changes the boost.

Explore the full lesson →

Phantom power

An electrical supply sent through a microphone cable by a mixer or audio interface. It is most commonly 48 volts, and many condenser microphones need it to operate.

Picture this: The interface supplies power to a condenser microphone through its XLR cable. Phantom power is not the amplifier power sent to speakers, and most ordinary dynamic microphones do not need it.

Volume control

The knob you normally turn to make music quieter or louder. It adjusts the signal level, but its position is not a measurement of power.

Picture this: Halfway around the knob does not mean half the amplifier’s watts. Your recording, source level and speakers all affect the result.

Input sensitivity

The signal level needed to produce a specified amplifier output.

Picture this: An amp rated 200 mV sensitivity is not necessarily overloaded by a 2 V source.

Headroom

Spare clean output available for a brief louder moment.

Picture this: The opening drum hit can need extra room above the music's average level.

Clipping

Distortion produced when a signal tries to go beyond a stage's clean output range.

Picture this: An overloaded amplifier may flatten the peaks of a loud note.

Distortion

An unintended change or addition to the reproduced signal.

Picture this: A rough loud passage could reflect distortion, but a sound alone is not a diagnosis.

THD

Picture this: Check the frequency, output level, and load alongside the printed percentage.

THD+N

Harmonic distortion and noise measured together relative to a test signal.

Picture this: This includes hiss or hum alongside added harmonics.

Noise floor

The underlying background noise in a recording or device.

Picture this: You may notice quiet hiss when nothing else is playing.

Damping factor

Picture this: A huge number is not proof of tighter bass.

Sound & music

Frequency

How many times a vibration or wave repeats each second.

Picture this: Bass notes vibrate more slowly than treble notes.

Hertz (Hz)

One repeating cycle per second.

Picture this: A 100 Hz note repeats 100 times per second.

Decibel (dB)

A logarithmic way of expressing a sound or signal level or change.

Picture this: The meaning depends on what's being measured; dB isn't automatically a loudness reading.

Explore the full lesson →

Amplitude

The size of a wave's movement or a signal's swing.

Picture this: On the same waveform scale, a taller wave usually means more signal level.

Explore the full lesson →

Frequency response

How strongly a speaker or device reproduces different frequencies.

Picture this: Extra bass on the graph can make a system sound warmer.

Explore the full lesson →

Fundamental

The base repeating frequency of a harmonic musical tone.

Picture this: A 100 Hz fundamental is its first harmonic.

Explore the full lesson →

Harmonics

The main note and higher components that repeat at whole-number multiples of it. The main note itself is the first harmonic.

Picture this: If the main note is 100 Hz, the first, second, third and fourth harmonics are 100, 200, 300 and 400 Hz. Those higher components help give an instrument its character.

Overtones

The parts of a sound higher than its main note. Unlike harmonics, the overtone count starts above that note.

Picture this: In a harmonic sound, the first overtone is the second harmonic, and the second overtone is the third harmonic. Bells can produce higher tones that are not exact multiples: overtones, but not harmonics.

Transient

A sudden change in a signal, often at the beginning of a sound.

Picture this: The initial crack of a snare drum is a transient.

Dynamic range

The level difference between quiet and loud portions of a recording.

Picture this: A whisper followed by a shouted chorus has strong contrast.

Compression

Reducing level differences in audio; also used differently for speakers that stop getting proportionally louder.

Picture this: A strong master can make quiet and loud parts closer in volume.

Phase

Where one repeating wave is in its cycle relative to another.

Picture this: Two waves can reinforce or partly cancel when mixed.

Phase angle

How far one repeating quantity leads or lags another.

Picture this: Speaker current and voltage need not rise and fall at precisely the same time.

Crossover

Picture this: A woofer handles bass while a tweeter handles higher notes.

How broadly a speaker sends sound in different directions.

Picture this: The sound can change as you move to the side of the couch.

A COMMON POINT OF CONFUSION

Harmonics versus overtones

Start with the main note. That is the first harmonic. The second, third and fourth harmonics vibrate two, three and four times as fast. For a 100 Hz note, those are 100, 200, 300 and 400 Hz.

Overtones start counting above the main note. In a harmonic sound, the first overtone is the second harmonic, and the second overtone is the third harmonic.

Why the difference? Harmonics must follow that exact multiples pattern. Overtones only have to be higher than the main note. A bell can ring with extra higher tones that are not exact multiples. They are overtones, but not harmonics. Explore added harmonics in amplifiers →

Digital audio

The number of waveform samples recorded each second.

Picture this: 44.1 kHz stores 44,100 samples per second for each channel.

Bit depth

The number of levels available for a standard digital PCM audio sample.

Picture this: Greater bit depth provides more potential recording dynamic range.

Explore the full lesson →

Bitrate

The amount of encoded audio data used per second.

Picture this: 320 kb/s MP3 refers to data rate, not listening volume.

Explore the full lesson →

Codec

A method for encoding and decoding audio.

Picture this: MP3, AAC, and FLAC represent audio using different methods.

Explore the full lesson →

Lossy

Data compression that discards some information.

Picture this: MP3 can be hard to distinguish from the original at sufficient bitrate, but discarded data is gone.

Explore the full lesson →

Lossless

Encoding that can reconstruct the source digital audio samples exactly.

Picture this: FLAC can decode back to the same PCM samples.

Explore the full lesson →

DAC

A digital-to-analogue converter that turns digital samples into an analogue signal.

Picture this: A CD player or sound card uses a DAC before its analogue output.

Explore the full lesson →

Aliasing

Unwanted false frequencies caused by insufficient sampling or filtering.

Picture this: Correct sampling and conversion filters help prevent them.
