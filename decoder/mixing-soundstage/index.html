LESSON 22 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 3

Who puts the band in your listening room?

When you hear a voice between the speakers, a guitar stretching left, or drums behind the singer, much of that spatial picture may have been deliberately built in the mix. The loudspeakers and room determine how convincingly you hear it, but the original recording and the engineer's choices provide the cues.

The central distinction: Recording captures or creates individual sounds. Mixing combines them into a performance with balance, space, tone and motion. Mastering then prepares and refines the finished mix. A more expensive amplifier cannot separate instruments that were irreversibly combined in a finished recording.

Balance and masking

An engineer can lower a loud guitar, adjust a vocal's presence range, automate a bass line or change the arrangement so fewer parts compete. Overlap on a frequency plot indicates only potential competition, not proven masking or audible congestion.

Depth, movement and energy

Dry versus reverberant balance, early reflections, delays and automation can create a sense of depth or motion. Transient shaping and bus compression affect perceived attack and dynamics, but the performance still matters. Not every mix benefits from maximum width.

02 / TIME, PHASE AND MONO

Two microphones can sound wide in stereo and cancel in mono.

If two microphones capture nearly the same sound at different times, combining them electrically can produce a comb filter. Our calculated example uses two equal, identical, perfectly correlated signals with a selectable delay. It illustrates a mathematical limit, not what any particular microphone pair will sound like.

Coincident XY and mid/side

These techniques can maintain useful mono compatibility, depending on how the microphones and matrices are configured. XY derives stereo primarily from level differences; mid/side lets the mixer adjust width by changing the side component.

Spaced microphones

A/B pairs create time and level differences that can suggest width. But time-offset signals can comb-filter when combined to mono, especially if they are strongly correlated. A stereo listening experience is not simply the electrical mono sum of left and right.

03 / IT ISN'T ALL THE SAME KIND OF STEREO

Binaural, QSound, 5.1 and Atmos are different tools.

Some techniques encode a spacious illusion into just two channels. Others deliver genuinely separate surround channels or object positions. Choose a format to see what the engineer actually produces and what playback it needs.

Two channels, usually two loudspeakers or headphones

Traditional stereo mixing

Panning changes the ratio sent to the left and right channels. A centered vocal is a phantom center when the two speakers are balanced and the listener is near the intended position.

Important distinction: Stereo doesn't inherently encode an independent sound above or behind the listener. Reverb, delays, microphone cues and processing can create impressions beyond the speakers, but perception depends on playback geometry.

Two channels designed around how the ears hear

Binaural capture or HRTF rendering

A dummy-head recording captures two ear-like signals using a head/ear acoustic structure. An HRTF renderer instead synthesizes ear-dependent time and spectral cues from separate signals or spatial metadata.

Important distinction: Often most directly experienced over headphones. Individual ears, head tracking, room and crossfeed matter; a standard stereo mix played on headphones is not automatically binaural. Some dummy-head recordings also work on loudspeakers.

Processed spatial cues carried in an ordinary stereo master

QSound-processed stereo

QSound-era mixes used signal processing to create spatial illusions while remaining compatible with conventional two-channel distribution.

Important distinction: It is not a separate 5.1 track or an objective measurement that a sound is physically located outside the speakers. Effects depend on the exact mix, the listening setup and level.

Dedicated front, surround and low-frequency-effects channels

Discrete 5.1-channel mixing

A 5.1 mix delivers separate channels to an appropriate surround system, which can reproduce independent side/rear material without relying only on two-channel spatial cues.

Important distinction: A receiver that upmixes stereo to 5.1 does not magically reveal a discrete 5.1 mix that was never present. DVD/Blu-ray releases may have different stereo, 5.1 and remastered editions.

Channel-based beds plus positioned audio objects with metadata

Dolby Atmos beds and objects

A mixer can use beds (fixed channel groups) and objects (audio with position metadata). A renderer adapts the result to compatible speaker arrays or a binaural headphone presentation.

Important distinction: Atmos on headphones is a rendered version and does not make a plain stereo master an independent original Atmos production. Bed/object counts and delivered formats depend on workflow and device.

The diagrams are generic schematics, not real Dolby renderer outputs, QSound algorithms, impulse responses or measured loudspeaker positions. Headphone spatial rendering depends on individual head and ear characteristics. A given recording may have multiple stereo, multichannel and Atmos mixes, not just differently mastered exports.

05 / TRANSLATING AUDIOPHILE LANGUAGE

Which adjectives might originate in the mix?

Soundstage / Imaging

Mic pickup, pan positions, room reverberation, stereo correlation and spatial processing determine which cues are in the master. Speaker placement and the listener's room determine how those cues unfold.

Separation / Congestion

Arrangement, levels, spectral masking, timing, saturation and bus dynamics influence whether simultaneous sources remain distinguishable. A good amplifier cannot unmash a flattened mix.

Forward / Warm / Bright

Relative voice/guitar levels, spectral shaping, mic proximity and reverberant balance may encourage these descriptions. They are not universal measurements or the exclusive properties of headphones or speakers.

Dynamic / Punchy

Performance, transient envelopes, compressor attack/release and automation affect impact. Mastering can alter it further; our next lesson will distinguish macro contrast, crest factor and loudness metrics.

The honest boundary: playback components can add measurable coloration or processing, and some DSP can construct new spatial effects. They cannot reliably recreate missing source tracks, the precise unrecorded room or transients deliberately removed from the final mix. A spacious mix still needs reasonably suitable playback for its spatial intentions to translate.
