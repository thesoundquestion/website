LESSON 22 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 3

Who puts the band in your listening room?

When you hear a voice between the speakers, a guitar stretching left, or drums behind the singer, much of that spatial picture may have been deliberately built in the mix. The loudspeakers and room determine how convincingly you hear it, but the original recording and the engineer's choices provide the cues.

The central distinction: Recording captures or creates individual sounds. Mixing combines them into a performance with balance, space, tone and motion. Mastering then prepares and refines the finished mix. A more expensive amplifier cannot separate instruments that were irreversibly combined in a finished recording.

Balance and masking

An engineer can lower a loud guitar, adjust a vocal's presence range, automate a bass line or change the arrangement so fewer parts compete. Overlap on a frequency plot indicates only potential competition, not proven masking or audible congestion.

Depth, movement and energy

Dry versus reverberant balance, early reflections, delays and automation can create a sense of depth or motion. Transient shaping and bus compression affect perceived attack and dynamics, but the performance still matters. Not every mix benefits from maximum width.

02 / TIMING AND MONO

Why can a wide mix sound thin in mono?

Picture two microphones on the same snare drum. Sound reaches the closer mic first. If the engineer puts one recording on the left and the other on the right, the snare may sound wide. Switch to mono and those left and right signals blend into one. The little timing difference can change the snare’s tone.

Look at one part of the sound at the same instant. Two wave peaks can reinforce each other. If a peak in one copy lines up with a dip in the other, that part gets weaker.

Phase cancellation is what engineers call that weakening. It can affect several pitches at once, which can make the snare sound hollow or lose some of its snap. A series of dips in the tone is called comb filtering.

Try it by ear. If you can switch a mix to mono, listen to the snare or a guitar as you switch back and forth. Listen for a change in the tone as well as the smaller sense of space. When recording, moving one mic or blending in less of it can help if the sound gets thin.
See which pitches get quieter

Try the two delays, both about a thousandth of a second or less. The dashed blue line shows the mics lined up. The gold line shows what you get when you blend the two recordings. Each dip is a part of the sound that got quieter. The low pitches are on the left and the high pitches are on the right.

Both copies line up. The gold line follows the blue reference, so the tone stays the same.

A delay of 0.25 ms makes the first dip near 2 kHz. That can change the snap of the snare.

With a 1 ms delay, the first dip moves down to around 500 Hz. That can take some body out of the snare.

The example blends two identical recordings at equal volume. Real microphones can hear different levels and room reflections, so the dips can be shallower.

03 / IT ISN’T ALL THE SAME STEREO

One sound, five ways to place it

Follow the same shaker hit from your listening seat. Switch between the examples to see where the hit plays, where it may seem to come from, and what creates that impression.

Pan the hit toward the right speaker

How it’s placed
The engineer sends more of the hit to the right channel than the left. That control is called panning.
What you may hear
From a seat between the speakers, the hit seems to come from the front-right, somewhere between them. Sending the same sound equally to both speakers can put it in the middle. That’s the phantom centre.
Playback
Two speakers or stereo headphones. The map shows the speaker version.

Give each ear its own version of the hit

How it’s placed
Two mics in a model head can record what each ear would hear. Software can also work out those differences in timing and tone.
What you may hear
The ear closer to this hit gets it first. The head and ears also change how it sounds. On headphones, those clues can make the hit seem to come from just behind your right shoulder.
Playback
Headphones keep the left and right ear signals separate.

Make the hit seem farther out than the speakers

How it’s placed
The engineer applies QSound to the hit and records the result into the left and right channels of the release.
What you may hear
The hit can seem to come from past the right speaker, even though the sound comes from the two speakers shown. Where you sit and how the speakers are set up can change the illusion.
Playback
Two speakers, with your seat centred between them.

Send the hit to a speaker beside you

How it’s placed
The engineer sends the hit to the right surround. A 5.1 mix has separate left, centre, right and two surround channels, plus a bass-effects channel.
What you may hear
You can hear the hit from beside or slightly behind your right shoulder because a speaker there plays it. Other sounds can stay up front at the same time.
Playback
A 5.1 mix played on five speakers and a subwoofer, as shown.

Move the hit from in front of you to above you

How it’s placed
The engineer can give the hit a position and change it over time. The sound and its position are stored together as an object. The rest of the mix can also use groups of channels called beds.
What you may hear
Here the hit repeats as it moves upward. A compatible player uses the speakers it has, including height speakers in this setup, to follow that move. It can also create a version for headphones.
Playback
An Atmos mix and compatible playback. The side view shows an example with height speakers.

Where you hear the hit depends on the mix and your listening setup, including your seat, room or headphones. The album examples below point to specific releases that use these approaches.

05 / TRANSLATING AUDIOPHILE LANGUAGE

Which adjectives might originate in the mix?

Soundstage / Imaging

Mic pickup, pan positions, room reverberation, stereo correlation and spatial processing determine which cues are in the master. Speaker placement and the listener's room determine how those cues unfold.

Separation / Congestion

Arrangement, levels, spectral masking, timing, saturation and bus dynamics influence whether simultaneous sources remain distinguishable. A good amplifier cannot unmash a flattened mix.

Forward / Warm / Bright

Relative voice/guitar levels, spectral shaping, mic proximity and reverberant balance may encourage these descriptions. They are not universal measurements or the exclusive properties of headphones or speakers.

Dynamic / Punchy

Performance, transient envelopes, compressor attack/release and automation affect impact. Mastering can alter it further; our next lesson will distinguish macro contrast, crest factor and loudness metrics.

The honest boundary: playback components can add measurable coloration or processing, and some DSP can construct new spatial effects. They cannot reliably recreate missing source tracks, the precise unrecorded room or transients deliberately removed from the final mix. A spacious mix still needs reasonably suitable playback for its spatial intentions to translate.
