LESSON 13 · SOURCES & DACS

Playback sources: from the recording to your amplifier.

Vinyl, CDs, sound cards, USB DACs and streamers are often compared as though they do the same job. Some store music, some read it, some convert it, and some do several of those things at once.

The useful question is not “Which format sounds best?” It's “What happens to the signal at each step, and where could something change?”

Medium or file
Where the recording is stored: a groove, a CD, a file or a network service.
Transport / player
Reads the recording, retrieves data or handles playback.
Converter / phono stage
Turns digital audio into analogue, or makes a cartridge signal suitable for an amplifier.
Amplifier
Raises an analogue signal's available power for speakers. It may also contain a DAC or phono preamp.
START WITH THE SIGNAL PATH A DAC is a function, not necessarily a separate box.

A CD player's RCA sockets and a computer sound card's RCA sockets can both carry analogue signals that have already passed through a DAC.

THE EQUIPMENT · WHAT EACH COMPONENT ACTUALLY DOES

Six ways to get music into an amplifier.

Pay attention to whether the equipment delivers raw cartridge output, analogue line-level audio or digital data. The connector alone does not tell you the whole story.

01 / ANALOGUE MEDIUM

Vinyl record

The stylus follows a modulated groove. The cartridge generates a small signal, then the phono stage supplies gain and RIAA playback equalization.

Look for: MM/MC cartridge compatibility, cartridge loading, correct tracking, rumble, surface noise and phono-stage accuracy.

Why the phono input matters

A normal AUX/CD/TAPE input does not add the gain or playback equalization needed by a typical moving-magnet cartridge. Some turntables have a built-in phono stage with a LINE/PHONO switch. Make sure you don't apply phono equalization twice.

02 / DIGITAL MEDIUM

CD player or transport

A standard audio CD carries two-channel 16-bit, 44.1 kHz PCM. A complete player contains a DAC for analogue RCA output. A transport can deliver digital data to another DAC.

Look for: whether the outputs are optical/coaxial digital, analogue RCA, or both; mechanical reliability; and the actual DAC/output measurements.

Can an external DAC improve a CD player?

Only if the connection and implementation solve a measurable or audible limitation. A functioning CD player with clean analogue output has already converted the data; adding another DAC is not automatically an upgrade.

03 / COMPUTER AUDIO

Internal sound card

Music software decodes audio. The operating system and driver may mix or resample it. The sound card's DAC produces an analogue output for a receiver or powered speakers.

Look for: analogue output voltage, noise, THD+N, driver/OS settings, clocking and the presence of unwanted DSP.

Does a sound card equal a cheap DAC?

No. A sound card is an audio interface that includes one or more DAC functions. A card with excellent analogue measurements can perform just as transparently as an external DAC. Installation, noise coupling and connection needs vary.

04 / EXTERNAL CONVERSION

USB DAC

A computer or compatible device sends digital audio over USB. The DAC converts it into analogue audio. Some units also include a headphone amplifier, volume control or ADC.

Look for: supported host/input formats, real output performance, RCA versus balanced voltage, output impedance and controls.

What does 32-bit / 768 kHz actually tell us?

It mostly describes an accepted input format, not the noise floor or sound quality of the analogue output. Bit depth and sample rate are meaningful concepts, but large advertised numbers are not a listening verdict.

05 / NETWORK PLAYBACK

Network streamer

A streamer receives music over a network, buffers or decodes it, and outputs either digital audio or analogue audio via an internal DAC.

Look for: local-file support, music-service limitations, audio-format support, app reliability, analogue-output measurements and usable connectivity.

Wi-Fi, Ethernet or Bluetooth?

Wi-Fi/Ethernet can carry lossless digital audio streams. Bluetooth audio often involves lossy codecs, but the exact result depends on the devices and codec. The connection method alone does not tell you whether two releases use the same master.

06 / OTHER ANALOGUE SOURCES

Cassette, reel-to-reel and tuner

These generally supply analogue line-level output after their own playback circuitry. Tape decks apply suitable playback equalization and gain internally.

Look for: tape speed, alignment, head condition, hiss, wow/flutter, noise reduction and source condition.

Are older sources automatically lower fidelity?

No universal verdict follows from age alone. Mechanical and analogue noise limitations are real, but maintenance, mastering and recording quality matter enormously, and a listener may prefer a particular presentation.

ACCURACY · COLOUR · PERSONAL PREFERENCE

Where could a source actually change the sound?

Changing the box or medium does not guarantee a change at your ears. Look for an identifiable mechanism and ask whether the magnitude is meaningful.

Potentially measurable changes

Vinyl: surface noise, wow/flutter, groove/tracing distortion, cartridge frequency response, channel separation and phono EQ error.

Digital hardware: DAC noise, distortion, filtering, output level, and sometimes output-impedance/load interactions.

Computer playback: resampling, system mixing, replay gain, clipping and intentionally enabled DSP.

Recorded programme: different mastering, level or dynamic-range treatment can overwhelm the small differences between competent playback devices.

For detailed measurements and listening implications, continue with DAC specifications, source levels, lossy versus lossless and subjective listening terminology.

CLAIM CHECK

“Vinyl is warm. Digital is cold.”

Neither medium has one inevitable tonal signature. A vinyl cartridge, its loading, tracking and mastering may create measurable differences. A digital release may use a different master.

When two sources are clean, level-matched and reproduce the same programme, a controlled comparison is more informative than adjectives or price tags.

Preference isn't an error. Enjoying a coloured presentation and accurately identifying its cause are separate questions.

INTERACTIVE 02 · VINYL PLAYBACK EQ

Why a record cartridge needs a phono stage.

During standard RIAA disc mastering, low frequencies are reduced and high frequencies boosted relative to a reference. Playback applies the complementary frequency shaping to restore the intended balance.

The orange curve shows the theoretical RIAA playback stage response relative to 1 kHz. The blue line shows a perfectly flat line-level stage for comparison.

This is not the overall response of a vinyl record, cartridge or turntable. The complete playback response also depends on the record, cartridge, loading, alignment and equalization accuracy.

Optional maths: the RIAA time constants

The idealized analogue playback response is proportional to (1 + s·318 µs) / [(1 + s·3180 µs)(1 + s·75 µs)], evaluated at s = j2πf and normalized to 0 dB at 1 kHz. These are the standard 3180, 318 and 75 µs time constants, corresponding to transition regions near 50, 500 and 2122 Hz.

Only the calculated ideal transfer function is plotted. A real phono stage will have finite tolerances and potentially additional low-frequency filtering.

INTERACTIVE 03 · CONNECT YOUR EQUIPMENT

Does your source need an extra box?

Choose the output you actually have and the amplifier input you intend to use. The checker identifies missing functions, not recommended brands or exact compatible cables.

A phono stage is missing

A raw moving-magnet cartridge needs a suitable MM phono preamp with RIAA playback equalization before an ordinary line input.

Illustrative compatibility logic only. Connector shape, ground arrangements, cartridge requirements, digital protocol support and available input gain must be verified in the actual equipment manuals. Start at low volume after changing connections.

Two easy mistakes to avoid

1. Do not connect a turntable's amplified LINE output to an MM/MC PHONO input unless the turntable's internal phono stage is switched off and the cartridge matches that input. 2. An optical or USB cable does not become an analogue RCA connection without the proper DAC hardware.

BUYING AND SETUP · WHAT MATTERS MOST

Which playback source should you actually use?

Start with connectivity

Check whether the amplifier takes RCA line, phono, USB audio, optical or coaxial digital. Buy the missing function, not an extra box for its own sake.

Compare matching measurements

Output level, frequency response with tolerance, noise, distortion, channel separation and load compatibility can be relevant. A high bit-depth figure alone proves little.

Keep the recording constant

If you compare CD, vinyl and streaming, check that they're using the same mastering and playback level. Different releases undermine a simple format-versus-format conclusion.

For your system, a PC with an X-Fi Titanium HD feeding an amplifier through its RCA outputs already contains the required DAC. You would need a separate converter only for a specific feature, connectivity or measurable performance reason, not because the converter is inside a computer.
