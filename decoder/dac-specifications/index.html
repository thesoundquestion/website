LESSON 14 · SOURCES & DACS

Can a DAC's specifications tell you how good it sounds?

Some DACs advertise huge signal-to-noise figures. Others emphasize 192 kHz playback. These numbers answer different questions. Start with what the measurements actually mean.

DAC
Digital-to-analogue converter: turns digital samples into an analogue electrical signal.
Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
The stated signal's level compared with measured background noise.
A-weighting
A filter applied to the measurement to weight frequencies in a specified way.

What do these numbers actually mean?

Understand the result

Output level describes how strong the DAC's analogue electrical signal is at a stated digital level and load. Frequency response describes how evenly pitches are reproduced. SNR compares the test signal to background noise. THD+N combines harmonic distortion and noise in a stated test.

SINAD (signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio) is another way to express the combined unwanted components relative to a signal. Under equivalent definitions, 0.001% THD+N corresponds to about 100 dB SINAD. Test frequency, output load, stimulus amplitude, measurement bandwidth and weighting matter before comparing products.

Support for 24-bit / 192 kHz data tells you what digital signals the DAC can accept. It does not measure its analogue noise, distortion or stereo imaging. Likewise, an impressive DAC chip rating does not guarantee identical performance from every finished product built around it.

Where does 6.3 microvolts come from? Optional maths

Convert the 110 dB voltage-level difference to a ratio: 10110/20 ≈ 316,228. Divide the 2 V RMS reference by that ratio: approximately 0.00000632 V RMS, or 6.32 µV RMS. This is A-weighted because the fictional published rating specifies A-weighting, and should not be substituted for an unweighted measurement.

What could you notice when listening?

Lower DAC noise can produce a quieter background if that DAC's noise was audible through the system. Sensitive headphones may expose noise that speakers in a normal room do not. If the existing DAC is already quiet under your listening conditions, improving SNR on paper may change nothing you can hear.

Terms such as “black background” describe an impression. A 120 dB SNR label cannot establish “holographic imaging,” more musicality or increased separation.

COMMON CLAIM

“A 120 dB SNR DAC must sound more detailed than one rated at 110 dB.”

WHAT CAN BE CONCLUDED

At equivalent reference, weighting and bandwidth, it has a lower noise measurement. Audibility and preference are separate questions.

What should you check?

  1. Compare the finished DAC product, not just the converter chip's possible best-case performance.
  2. Read noise and THD+N alongside their test levels, output load, weighting and bandwidth.
  3. Check its analogue output voltage and impedance, connections and matching to your amplifier. See source-level matching.
  4. Decide whether the new device fixes an audible or functional limitation rather than just raising a number on the specification sheet.
