LESSON 21 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 2

Why do recording engineers use 24-bit and higher sample rates?

A final 16-bit / 44.1 kHz CD can reproduce music remarkably well. So why do studios often record at 24-bit, work at 48 or 96 kHz, and process audio using 32-bit floating point? Because capturing, editing and playing back a signal are different jobs.

The short answer: More recording bit depth gives room to leave analogue-to-digital peaks safely below clipping without quantization noise becoming the first problem. Higher working sample rates can help some effects avoid aliasing. Neither automatically gives finished playback a wider soundstage or more musical dynamics.

01 / FOLLOW THE SIGNAL

First, identify where the limits actually live.

A microphone can distort before the preamp. A preamp can overload before the ADC. A digital file can clip if an integer converter runs out of range. And a DAW can process a clean recording internally at much greater numerical headroom.

0 dBFS

The maximum normal sample magnitude of fixed-point PCM. A waveform over this input limit cannot be represented without clipping at a conventional ADC.

Bits

Control quantization precision and the theoretical noise floor of integer PCM, not how powerful the singer is or how dynamic a mix is artistically.

Samples/second

Control the bandwidth of an appropriately filtered digital representation and affect certain processing choices, not the number of points a DAC joins with straight lines.

Clipped tops are flattened in the recorded signal. Simply turning the file down later makes the distortion quieter; it does not restore the missing waveform.

For an unpredictable vocal or drum performance, peaks around −12 dBFS can be a useful starting point, not a mandatory standard. Actual input gain also determines how microphone, preamp and converter noise relate to the captured signal. A “safe” digital meter cannot save an analogue preamp that has already clipped.

16-bit PCM≈ 98.1 dB ideal full-scale sine SNR−18 dBFS peak → ≈ 80.1 dB theoretical quantization margin relative to that peak
24-bit PCM≈ 146.2 dB ideal full-scale sine SNR−18 dBFS peak → ≈ 128.2 dB theoretical quantization margin relative to that peak

Those 98/146 dB values come from the ideal quantization relation 6.02 × bits + 1.76, which assumes a full-scale sine. The “margin relative to the peak” is an idealized illustration, not a promised real microphone, preamp or ADC SNR. Practical analogue noise often dominates well before 24-bit quantization noise does.

03 / FLOATING-POINT AUDIO

32-bit float is not a universal undo button.

The word float can describe a DAW’s numerical processing, a WAV file’s representation, or a specialized recorder engineered with multiple converters. Those are not the same hardware capability. Select the place where overload occurs.

A fixed-point converter whose input exceeds its analogue/full-scale range cannot encode the original peak. Saving it as 32-bit float afterward preserves only a distorted waveform.

Result: After −6 dB gain, the clipped plateau remains. The original peak cannot be reconstructed from this recording.

Unclipped capture, boost inside the DAW: recoverable

Clean ADC→DAW floating-point boost above 0 dBFS→lower output gain

Intended signal Stored/adjusted signal

A properly captured 24-bit PCM waveform can be boosted above nominal 0 dBFS inside a floating-point audio engine. If no plugin, bus or fixed-point export clips it, you can lower gain again.

Result: After −6 dB gain, the waveform remains curved instead of flat-topped. The same benefit depends on the floating-point path remaining intact.

Specialized multi-ADC hardware: wider input range

Suitable analogue front end→multiple conversion stages→32-bit float WAV

Intended signal Stored/adjusted signal

Some field recorders combine high-dynamic-range preamps and multiple ADC paths so loud and soft signals can be retained over a wider usable input range. The file format alone does not provide those electronics.

Result: A post-recording gain adjustment may bring over-reference samples into range, provided the microphone and analogue circuits did not overload.

WHERE DOES THE OVERLOAD OCCUR?

Physical analogue overload: still distorted in float

Microphone capsule or preamp saturates→ADC→32-bit float WAV

Intended signal Stored/adjusted signal

A capsule, wireless transmitter, transformer or preamp may distort before the ADC. Even a state-of-the-art float recorder can faithfully store that already-distorted waveform.

Result: Turning down the floating-point file cannot undo a clipped microphone diaphragm or overloaded preamp.

The important test: Did the microphone, preamp, analogue input and ADC actually capture the waveform without overloading? A file can faithfully store distortion too. Real recorder designs, analogue dynamic range and digital signal routing matter more than a “32-bit” badge.

04 / WHY MORE SAMPLES CAN HELP THE ENGINEER

Oversampling can stop a new, unwanted tone from appearing.

Here is a concrete technical reason some productions use a higher internal processing rate. An intentionally nonlinear effect adds harmonics. With a 10 kHz tone, the third harmonic falls at 30 kHz. That frequency is above the 24 kHz Nyquist limit of a 48 kHz processing rate.

SPECTRUM OVERLAYS

10 kHz source 18 kHz alias (unwanted)

The 10 kHz tone is below Nyquist, so the input itself is fine. A nonlinear effect generates a 30 kHz third harmonic after the ADC. At 48 kHz sampling, 30 kHz lies above the 24 kHz Nyquist limit: it can reappear as a false 18 kHz component. This demonstration assumes a naive nonlinear stage without effective antialias filtering.

10 kHz source + NONLINEAR THIRD HARMONIC

30 kHz stays at 30 kHz until proper filtering

10 kHz source 30 kHz newly created harmonic, before delivery filtering

At 96 kHz, Nyquist is 48 kHz, so the effect's 30 kHz harmonic can exist without folding to 18 kHz. Before converting back to 48 kHz, a suitable low-pass filter removes the out-of-band 30 kHz component. The delivered 48 kHz file can be clean without retaining ultrasonic audio.

Delivery: filter the 30 kHz harmonic before returning the audio to a 48 kHz file. The output need not contain ultrasonic frequencies.

10 kHz source + NONLINEAR THIRD HARMONIC

A 48 kHz project can achieve the same benefit internally

10 kHz source 30 kHz harmonic at the plugin’s internal rate, before filtering

A plugin oversamples internally to 96 kHz, runs the nonlinear effect at that working rate, filters new frequencies above the 48 kHz delivery band and downsamples. You need not record the entire session at 96 kHz for an oversampled processor to reduce this artifact.

Delivery: filter the 30 kHz harmonic before returning the audio to a 48 kHz file. The output need not contain ultrasonic frequencies.

48 kHz processing30 kHz → 48 − 30 = 18 kHz alias96 kHz processing30 kHz remains at 30 kHz until filtered

The chart shows possible spectral locations, not a measured distortion spectrum or a guaranteed audible difference. A real effect may create other harmonics and distortion; good oversampling requires appropriate interpolation and antialias filtering. Higher rates cost CPU and storage, and some already well-designed plugins make their own internal oversampling decisions.

Another specialized case: extreme pitch shifting

Suppose a microphone, source and ADC genuinely capture a 30 kHz ultrasonic component at 96 kHz. Shift that recorded material down an octave and the component becomes 15 kHz. That can be useful for unusual sound design. It is not proof that 30 kHz must be retained for normal listening, and many real sources/mics have little usable signal at that frequency.

05 / PRACTICAL RECORDING SETTINGS

What should a beginner actually choose?

JobPractical starting pointReason
Recording a band, voice or acoustic instrument24-bit PCM · 44.1 or 48 kHzExcellent working headroom and normal audible bandwidth; prioritise gain, room, mic and performance.
Video production or mixed audio/video project24-bit PCM · 48 kHzCommon production convention, simple file handling and plenty of recording headroom.
Nonlinear processing, sound design, extreme pitch manipulation24-bit PCM · 96 kHz when beneficialExtra working bandwidth for certain effects or pitch moves; 48 kHz project with oversampled plugins can often suffice.
Unpredictable field recording with suitable hardware32-bit float recorder if its analogue/ADC design benefits the useCan reduce gain-setting risk; will not recover distortion before its clean input range.
DAW processing / exchange between engineers32-bit float export where supported and appropriatePreserves intermediate numerical headroom; final delivery can use a different word length.
Finished playbackUse the released master in its native supported formatThe quality of performance, mix and mastering usually matters more than an unsupported “high-res always wins” claim.
