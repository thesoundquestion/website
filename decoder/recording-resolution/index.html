LESSON 21 · RECORDING & PRODUCTION · PART 2

Why do recording engineers use 24-bit and higher sample rates?

A singer hits a louder note in the take than at soundcheck. The right input level gives that note room before it turns into a crackle. The file settings affect what you can adjust later.

24-bit recording lets you leave room for loud notes while keeping the errors from rounding each level reading low. 32-bit float gives you more room to adjust a clean track. A higher sample rate can help some effects avoid adding stray pitches.

01 / FOLLOW THE SIGNAL

Where can a loud note get cut off?

A loud note goes through several pieces of gear before you see it on the computer. A mic or its amplifier can already sound rough by the time the recording meter shows the level. Once the take is captured cleanly, the software usually has much more room to adjust its volume.

0 dBFS is the top of the meter

It means full scale, the biggest level a standard recording file can store. A peak at −12 dBFS is 12 dB below that limit. That room for a louder sound is called headroom.

Bit depth sets how finely levels are stored

24-bit uses finer steps than 16-bit. Both have a 0 dBFS ceiling. Read more about bit depth.

A higher rate means more readings each second

At 48 kHz, the recorder takes 48,000 readings per second. That rate sets the highest pitch it can store correctly. See how sample rate works.

See the bit-depth calculation

In an ideal digital recording, a steady tone that reaches full scale has a signal-to-noise ratio of about 6.02 × bits + 1.76 dB. The number compares the tone with the small errors from rounding. Lower the same tone by 18 dB and its lead over those errors also drops by 18 dB.

16-bitAbout 98 dB at full scaleAbout 80 dB with the tone peaking at −18 dBFS
24-bitAbout 146 dB at full scaleAbout 128 dB with the tone peaking at −18 dBFS

These are calculated values for a pure test tone. An actual recording also contains noise from the room, mic and interface.

03 / FLOATING-POINT AUDIO

When can turning down a track save the take?

32-bit float is a way for software and some recording files to store very big and very small numbers. If you captured a clean vocal and turned it up too far in the software, you may be able to turn it back down. If the mic or recorder already cut off the peaks, those flat spots are part of the take.

Choose where the sound gets too loud. In each example the gold line shows the track after you turn it down by 6 dB, about half the height of the wave. Look at the shape of the tops and bottoms.

Some field recorders use separate converters for quieter and louder signals. That gives them room to capture a whisper and a shout in the same take. The 32-bit float file can hold the range of levels the hardware captured.

04 / HIGHER RATES INSIDE AN EFFECT

Why can an effect add the wrong pitch?

A distortion effect changes the shape of a sound and can add pitches related to the original. Those pitches are called harmonics. Here the effect starts with a 10 kHz test tone and creates one at three times that pitch, or 30 kHz.

At a 48 kHz working rate, the highest pitch the software can represent correctly is just below 24 kHz. If an effect tries to create a pitch above that limit, it can show up as a different pitch below the limit. That’s aliasing. In the graph, follow where the second mark appears as you change the setting.

SPECTRUM OVERLAYS

The extra tone shows up at 18 kHz

Original 10 kHz tone Unwanted 18 kHz tone

The effect tries to add a 30 kHz tone, which is 6 kHz above the 24 kHz limit. It shows up 6 kHz below the limit, at 18 kHz. Once that 18 kHz tone is there, a filter set to remove tones above 24 kHz leaves it behind.

At 96 kHz, the extra tone stays at 30 kHz

Original 10 kHz tone Added 30 kHz tone, before filtering

Working at 96 kHz raises the limit to 48 kHz, so the 30 kHz tone can be represented at its own pitch. Before saving a 48 kHz file, the software can filter out that extra tone.

Only the effect needs to work faster

Original 10 kHz tone Added 30 kHz tone inside the effect, before filtering

An effect can briefly work at twice the project rate, then filter out high pitches and return to 48 kHz. This is called oversampling. It gives the effect more room to work while the rest of the project stays at 48 kHz.

The markers show where the tones would land. Whether you could hear a stray tone in a real mix depends on its pitch and level. A higher project rate also uses more storage and processing power.

Why a sound designer might record at 96 kHz

Say you want to lower a recorded sound by an octave to make an effect. That halves all its frequencies. If your mic and recorder picked up a tone at 30 kHz, it would move to 15 kHz after the pitch shift. That’s a reason to use a higher recording rate for this particular job.

05 / PRACTICAL RECORDING SETTINGS

What settings should you start with?

What you’re doingStarting settingWhat it gives you
Recording a voice, instrument or band24-bit at 44.1 or 48 kHzRoom to set the recording level below the top of the meter. Spend time on mic position and the sound in the room.
Recording audio for video24-bit at 48 kHzMatches the rate commonly used for video.
Using distortion effects or making big pitch changes24-bit at 48 kHz to start. Try oversampling in the effect or 96 kHz for a specific need.Gives the effect more room to work. A higher recording rate can help if you plan to shift captured high pitches down.
Recording sounds with sudden level changesA recorder designed for 32-bit floatCan handle a wider range of input levels, within what the mic and recorder can capture cleanly.
Sending tracks for someone else to mix32-bit float if their software supports it and they want that formatKeeps more room for level changes while the mix is still in progress.
Listening to a finished releaseUse the release in a format your gear supportsA standard CD plays at 16-bit and 44.1 kHz. If you’re comparing releases, check which mix or master each one uses.

How much space does a recording take?

16-bit · 44.1 kHz10.6 MB/min
24-bit · 48 kHz17.3 MB/min
24-bit · 96 kHz34.6 MB/min
24-bit · 192 kHz69.1 MB/min

These sizes are for one minute of uncompressed stereo. At the same bit depth, doubling the sample rate doubles the space it uses. A lossless file such as FLAC can save space and keep the same audio data.
