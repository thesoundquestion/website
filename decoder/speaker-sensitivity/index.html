LESSON 03 · LOUDSPEAKERS · SOUND PER AMPLIFIER EFFORT

Why does one speaker play louder than another at the same setting?

Sensitivity explains the volume difference you can get from the same test input, and why the printed number can mislead.

YOUR EXAMPLE SPECIFICATION, EXPLAINED

Decode the example: 87 dB at 2.83 V / 1 m

Illustrative diagram, not a manufacturer's measured response or verification of a product.

87 dB is the claimed output level; 2.83 V is the test input; 1 m is the distance.

In the specified test setup, 2.83 volts applied to the speaker produces a stated sound-pressure level of 87 decibels measured at one metre. That is an output rating under a defined input and distance, not a frequency-response graph.

Check the input units. At the same 2.83 V, Ohm’s law gives about 0.35 A into 8 Ω and 0.71 A into 4 Ω. Multiplying volts by amps gives about 1 W and 2 W for those resistive loads. Compare sensitivity at the same input and distance.

Higher sensitivity

More sound from the same input

With the same test power and at the same distance, this speaker produces more sound. It takes less amplifier power to reach an equivalent measured level.

That may help in a larger room or with a lower-power amplifier. It does not prove better bass, lower distortion or a nicer sound.

The headline figure is useful when the comparison conditions really match.

Lower sensitivity

Needs more input for the same output

This speaker produces less sound from the same electrical power. You may need to turn the amplifier up further to reach the same level.

It could still sound wonderful and be the better speaker for your music. A speaker designed for deeper bass, a different enclosure or lower distortion may trade away sensitivity.

Lower sensitivity isn't automatically inferior sound quality.

Output limiting

Stops keeping up as power increases

In this invented example, the speaker initially gets louder as power increases but eventually gains much less sound than expected. That is a different problem from low sensitivity.

Big drum hits may fail to grow as strongly as expected, or the speaker may sound strained at demanding levels. A real assessment would require distortion and compression tests.

A sensitivity number alone cannot reveal this limiting behavior.

FIRST, THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

Sensitivity is a rough indication of how much sound a speaker makes from a fixed test signal, at a defined distance. Think of two flashlights given the same amount of electricity: one might produce more light. That does not automatically mean it makes better light.

When two speakers are measured in the same way, a higher sensitivity number means more sound for the same reference input. That's useful when deciding how much amplifier headroom you might need.

But this is not the maximum output a speaker can deliver, and it cannot tell you how the speaker's bass, voices or high notes are balanced.

CONNECT THE GRAPH TO THE MUSIC

What could you actually hear?

A highly sensitive speaker could get loud with a relatively modest amplifier, while a lower-sensitivity speaker might need more amplifier power to reach the same listening level.

An amp's volume dial does not have a universal meaning between systems. The actual sound also depends on the source level, amplifier gain, distance and the room.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

Some enthusiasts describe sensitive speakers as more lively or immediate. Their impression may be real, but the sensitivity number does not, by itself, prove better dynamics or response to quick musical changes.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

Sensitivity predicts output level at a defined test point. It says little about how cleanly a speaker plays very loudly. When one manufacturer uses 2.83 volts and another uses one watt, an apparently direct comparison can be unfair without checking impedance.

TAKE THIS TO THE SHOP

Before you decide what to buy

  1. Compare figures quoted at the same distance and with matching input conventions.
  2. Check speaker impedance and how much power or voltage the amplifier can supply.
  3. Do not confuse the sensitivity rating with the speaker's maximum clean sound level.

What else can change the outcome?

Nominal and actual impedance, power versus voltage reference, room, distance, gain, how the response is averaged, and where loud-speaker compression begins.

Want the engineering explanation?Advanced detail

A +3 dB level change requires roughly twice the electrical power if speaker behavior remains linear. At 2.83 V, a 4 Ω test resistor draws twice the current and takes twice the watts of an 8 Ω resistor, about 2 W versus 1 W. The plotted curves are idealized 1 W / 1 m examples, except the third which is a fictional compression illustration. Real speaker efficiency changes with frequency and level.
