Ohm’s law for audio

Same voltage, fewer ohms, more amps. Ohms tell you how hard it is for current to flow.

Same 4 volts, two loads

The load is what the amp is connected to. Here the amp sends 4 volts to each test resistor in turn.

Load (ohms)Current (amps)Power (watts)
8 Ω 0.5 A 2 W
4 Ω 1 A 4 W

Why the amp has to work harder

In this test, watts are volts times amps. With the same voltage and twice the current, the 4-ohm load gets twice the power.

The power supply has to keep up, and the circuitry that drives the speaker has to carry the extra current. If the amp reaches its limit, the sound can distort or the amp can shut down to protect itself.

Check what the amp delivers into your speaker’s ohm rating and how low the speaker’s impedance can dip.

Work out the amps and watts Optional

Amps = volts ÷ ohms. For the 8 Ω test load, 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5 A. For the 4 Ω test load, 4 ÷ 4 = 1 A.

Watts = volts × amps for these resistive loads. At 4 V, that is 2 W into 8 Ω and 4 W into 4 Ω.

At a fixed 8 Ω, raising the voltage from 4 to 8 V doubles the current from 0.5 to 1 A. Power rises from 2 to 8 W because both voltage and current doubled.

The test uses RMS voltage and current, the effective values for a changing signal. Real speakers have different impedance at different notes. If voltage and current are out of step, their phase difference also affects the watts delivered.

Where the voltage goes at a headphone jack Optional

In this example, a jack starts at 1.00 V and has 10 Ω of resistance inside it. Connect a 32 Ω headphone and the same current flows through both parts.

  1. Add the ohms. 10 + 32 = 42 Ω.
  2. Divide volts by total ohms. 1.00 ÷ 42 ≈ 0.0238 A.
  3. Multiply that current by each part’s resistance. About 0.24 V drops inside the jack and 0.76 V reaches the headphones.

With fewer headphone ohms, more current flows and the jack drops more voltage. See what happens on two different notes.
