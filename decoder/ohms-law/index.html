Ohm’s law for audio

At the same voltage, a 4 Ω load draws twice as much current as an 8 Ω load. That changes what the amplifier has to supply and how many watts reach the load.

Volts (V)
The electrical difference the amplifier applies across the speaker or headphones.
Amps (A)
How much electric charge flows per second. This is current.
Ohms (Ω)
How strongly the connected device opposes current. For a music signal, this is impedance.
Watts (W)
How much electrical energy is delivered per second. This is power.

Hold the voltage steady. Change the load.

The amplifier applies 4 V RMS across each example load. RMS is the effective voltage of the changing test signal. The load is what you connect to the amplifier, and here we use simple resistors.

Current and power at 4 V RMS
LoadCurrentPower
8 Ω0.50 A 2 W
4 Ω1.00 A 4 W

To find amps, divide volts by ohms. In the first row, 4 ÷ 8 gives 0.50 A. In the second row, 4 ÷ 4 gives 1.00 A. Half the resistance takes twice the current at the same voltage.

That is why a speaker with a dip in its impedance can ask the amplifier for more current at that frequency. The amplifier must be able to sustain the voltage and supply the current for the example to hold.

Multiply volts by amps to get watts

For the resistive examples, the first row above gives 4 V × 0.50 A = 2 W. The second gives 4 V × 1.00 A = 4 W. The voltage stays the same and the current doubles, so the watts double.

Now keep the 8 Ω load and turn the signal voltage up. The current rises too, so both numbers in the power calculation change.

What changes when voltage doubles into 8 Ω
VoltageCurrentPower
4 V RMS0.50 A2 W
8 V RMS1.00 A8 W

At the same resistance, twice the voltage gives four times the watts. An amplifier’s power rating tells you the load and conditions under which it can deliver its rated output.

Where does the voltage go at a headphone jack?

Imagine a jack with 10 Ω of internal output impedance and 1.00 V RMS available before you connect anything. Plug in a 32 Ω headphone. In this simplified model, the same current flows through the jack’s internal resistance and the headphone.

  1. Add the two resistances. 10 Ω + 32 Ω = 42 Ω.
  2. Divide the available voltage by the total. 1.00 V ÷ 42 Ω ≈ 0.0238 A.
  3. Multiply that current by each resistance. About 0.24 V drops across the jack’s resistance and 0.76 V reaches the headphone.

Lower headphone impedance allows more current to flow through the jack’s resistance. More current makes a larger voltage drop inside the jack. If the headphone impedance changes from one note to the next, the loss can change too. Compare a bass note and a midrange note in the headphone lesson.

Formula reference

To findUseIn words
CurrentI = V ÷ RVolts divided by ohms
VoltageV = I × RAmps times ohms
ResistanceR = V ÷ IVolts divided by amps
Power in a resistorP = V × IVolts times amps

Use the voltage across the part you’re calculating. The voltage before the headphones are plugged in and the voltage across them can be different.

RMS, frequency and real loads Optional

RMS is the effective value of an alternating signal. Across a resistor, 4 V RMS delivers the same average power as a steady 4 V. Combining Ohm’s law with the power equation gives P = V²/R and P = I²R, using RMS voltage and current for these audio examples.

A speaker or headphone’s impedance can vary with frequency. For a sine wave at one frequency, the RMS current magnitude is the RMS voltage at the terminals divided by the impedance magnitude at that frequency. If the current and voltage are out of phase, average real power also depends on their phase difference.
