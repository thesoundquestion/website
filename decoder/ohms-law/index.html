Ohm’s law for audio

A chain reaction in the numbers: same voltage, fewer ohms, more amps. Ohms tell you how hard it is for current to flow.

ELECTRICITY WITHOUT THE EQUATIONS

One pump. Two taps. Different demands.

Imagine the amplifier as a pump. At the same pressure (voltage), a wider bathtub tap demands more water (current) than a narrow sink tap. But opening the tap wider cannot make the pump more powerful. If the load demands more current than the amplifier can safely supply at that voltage, the voltage may fall, the sound may distort, or current and thermal protection may limit or shut down the output. An underspecified or faulty amplifier pushed too hard can overheat or suffer damage; severe clipping or excessive output can also stress speakers. Protection helps, but is not a promise that nothing can fail.

Same 8 volts, two loads

The load is the electrical thing an amplifier is driving. In this teaching example, imagine connecting one simple 8-ohm resistor and then a 4-ohm resistor, asking the amplifier to keep the same 8 volts across each. These resistors stand in for the speaker's electrical demand; real speakers are more complicated.

Load (ohms)Current (amps)Power (watts)
8 Ω 1 A 8 W
4 Ω 2 A 16 W

Why the amp has to work harder

In this test, watts are volts times amps. With the same voltage and twice the current, the 4-ohm load gets twice the power.

At the same voltage, the lower load asks the output devices and power supply for more current. The table shows the ideal demand, not power a limited amplifier is guaranteed to deliver. An amplifier with only a 1.5 A safe limit cannot hold 8 V across the 4 Ω test load: it may reduce output, distort, limit current or activate protection. Sustained overload can overheat or damage an inadequate amplifier, and excessive/clipped output can threaten speakers.

Check what the amp delivers into your speaker’s ohm rating and how low the speaker’s impedance can dip.

Work out the amps and watts Optional

Amps = volts ÷ ohms. For the 8 Ω test load, 8 ÷ 8 = 1 A. For the 4 Ω test load, 8 ÷ 4 = 2 A (ideal demand).

Watts = volts × amps for these resistive loads. At 8 V, that would be 8 W into 8 Ω and 16 W into 4 Ω if the amplifier can sustain the required current.

At a fixed 8 Ω, raising the voltage from 4 to 8 V doubles the current from 0.5 to 1 A. Power rises from 2 to 8 W because both voltage and current doubled.

These examples use RMS voltage and current, a way of describing their effective strength when they change over time. Real speakers do not behave like fixed resistors: their impedance changes with pitch. Voltage and current can also rise and fall at different times (a phase difference), changing the actual power delivered.

Where the voltage goes at a headphone jack Optional

In this example, a jack starts at 1.00 V and has 10 Ω of resistance inside it. Connect a 32 Ω headphone and the same current flows through both parts.

  1. Add the ohms. 10 + 32 = 42 Ω.
  2. Divide volts by total ohms. 1.00 ÷ 42 ≈ 0.0238 A.
  3. Multiply that current by each part’s resistance. About 0.24 V drops inside the jack and 0.76 V reaches the headphones.

With fewer headphone ohms, more current flows and the jack drops more voltage. See what happens on two different notes.
