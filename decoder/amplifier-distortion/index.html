LESSON 05 · AMPLIFIERS · EXTRA SOUND THAT WASN'T IN THE RECORDING

What happens when an amplifier changes the music as it gets louder?

See distortion curves, understand clipping, and explore what audiophiles mean by second- and third-order harmonics.

Example spec: 0.05% at a stated outputOriginal teaching illustrations · Not real equipment tests

FIRST, THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

Imagine a guitar playing a clear note. An amplifier is meant to make that electrical signal stronger, but it can also add tiny extra tones that weren't present at that level in the original.

A distortion percentage attempts to measure some of those changes. It does not tell the whole story: the same overall number can come from different kinds of added tones, and the number can change as the volume goes up.

Near its limits, an amplifier may stop reproducing the peaks of the signal cleanly. When the top and bottom of the waveform are restricted, this is called clipping, and the character of the added sound can change dramatically.

Stays clean longer

Low added sound until near its limit

This version keeps the test's extra tones relatively low over most of the range, then rises sharply near the top. That sharp climb can indicate clipping or limiting.

You may hear little or no difference at comfortable levels, but pushing beyond the clean range can make loud passages rough or harsh.

A low THD curve doesn't prove two amplifiers will sound identical. It is one piece of evidence.

Different harmonic mix

Same total, different added tones

Imagine two amplifiers reporting the same THD number, but one has more second-harmonic and the other more third-harmonic content. A single percentage hides that distinction.

Some enthusiasts prefer certain coloration. Whether you could hear it depends on the level, the music, and the rest of the system. The curve shown here cannot describe harmonic type by itself.

This is a conceptual explanation of what THD leaves out, not a claim that one harmonic is always pleasant.

Early rise

Extra sound grows much sooner

Here, distortion begins rising while the amplifier still has plenty of advertised power left. Compared with the first example, this is a reason to look closer.

If the added sound becomes strong enough, louder music may lose clarity or sound strained. Whether it is audible depends on both the amount and character of distortion.

One test tone does not show every possible issue, so use more than one measurement when judging a real product.

CONNECT THE GRAPH TO THE MUSIC

What could you actually hear?

At extremely low levels, different harmonic mixtures may not be noticeable in music at all. At greater levels, especially with simple sustained tones, coloration may become easier to hear.

The tone that seems “smooth” to one listener might seem dull or altered to another. It is safer to compare actual recordings at matched levels than to infer a listening experience from a single THD figure.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“Second harmonic is warm and musical; third harmonic is harsh.” That familiar explanation contains a useful starting idea, but it isn't a universal rule. Both added tones are musically related to the original note, and audibility depends on more than the order.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

A second harmonic of a 200 Hz note appears at 400 Hz; a third appears at 600 Hz. Their measured levels and combinations matter. The sum called THD loses information about which harmonics dominate, how the distortion changes with frequency and power, and what happens with several musical notes playing together. Tube or transistor labels do not supply that missing evidence.

ONE NUMBER, TWO POSSIBLE SOUNDS

Second versus third harmonic: what changes?

Play a clean test note at 200 Hz. If the amplifier adds a second harmonic, an extra tone appears one octave higher at 400 Hz. A third harmonic appears at 600 Hz. The percentage called THD rolls different extra tones into one figure.

The bar lengths show relative emphasis within each invented distortion pattern, not measured sound levels. In this illustration the combined distortion amount could be matched even though the harmonic mixtures differ.

A common claim is that H2 creates warmth while H3 creates hardness. That is not a universal rule: the levels, listening material, other distortion components and audible thresholds all matter. Music can also hide low-level harmonics. Purposefully added saturation in music production is a different choice from an unwanted hi-fi playback error.

TAKE THIS TO THE SHOP

Before you decide what to buy

  1. Check the distortion figure's output power, load and test frequency.
  2. Prefer a curve across output power to one isolated percentage, when available.
  3. Treat claims about warm second-order or harsh third-order distortion as hypotheses unless an actual level and spectrum are provided.

What else can change the outcome?

Output level, frequency, speaker load, recording complexity, harmonic levels, intermodulation, amplifier circuit and feedback, ear sensitivity, and any distortion intentionally added during production.

Want the engineering explanation?Advanced detail

THD is a ratio of harmonic components to the fundamental, expressed as a percentage. THD+N also includes noise in the defined measurement bandwidth. Harmonic order is the integer multiplier of the original test tone: H2=2×, H3=3×. A conventional THD-versus-power graph commonly uses a logarithmic vertical axis; the example displayed here is entirely synthetic.
