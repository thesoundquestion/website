ANOTHER VERY COMMON AMPLIFIER MEASUREMENT

THD versus THD+N: what does the “+N” change?

THD measures harmonic distortion: extra tones an amplifier adds at multiples of the original test note. THD+N includes those harmonics plus noise, such as hiss and electrical hum. They're related measurements, but not interchangeable numbers.

THD: added harmonicsTHD+N: harmonics + noise

Original hypothetical curves, not real amplifier measurements. In this simplified example, noise remains fixed at 80 µV RMS, the test note is 1 kHz, the load is an ideal 8 Ω resistor, and harmonic distortion rises only near the output limit.

01 · QUIET OUTPUT

Noise takes up a bigger share

When music is playing quietly, even a small, steady background hiss can be a comparatively large part of the weak test signal. The THD+N percentage can look higher, even though the amount of hiss hasn't increased.

02 · MODERATE OUTPUT

The two curves move closer

As the signal gets stronger, the same amount of noise becomes a smaller percentage of it. THD+N may fall toward the THD value even though the amplifier hasn't suddenly become quieter in absolute terms.

03 · NEAR ITS LIMIT

Harmonic distortion can take over

As the amplifier approaches clipping or another output limit, distortion may rise sharply. Both readings can then increase together because harmonics, rather than background noise, dominate.

What audiophiles say

“THD+N is the more honest number.” It often provides a useful overall check of unwanted output, but one percentage still doesn't identify whether the result comes from harmonics, hum, random hiss, or some combination.

What the evidence tells us

The measured noise depends on the measurement bandwidth and filters used. A reading from 20 Hz–20 kHz cannot automatically be compared with one using a much wider bandwidth. A cleaner-looking THD figure isn't proof that noise is low, and a higher THD+N figure isn't automatically audible.

When comparing equipment: check whether the manufacturer reports THD or THD+N, along with test frequency, output level, load, analyzer bandwidth and any filters or weighting. Look for a THD+N versus power curve if you want to know how the result changes with listening level.
Why do the percentages behave this way? Technical detail

In a simplified case, when harmonic components and background noise are uncorrelated, THD+N is roughly the square root of THD² plus the squared noise-to-signal ratio, with both expressed as percentages. The same fixed output noise voltage represents a larger fraction of a lower signal voltage. Real THD+N measurements may also include other residual signals such as hum, and analyzer details affect what counts. This graph is for learning, not a prediction of an actual amplifier.

A COMMON SHOPPING QUESTION

What those ratings still leave unanswered

The graph comparisons above are not interchangeable tests. A single result, a maximum allowed percentage, and a rated-power condition all answer different questions. What else would you need to know to make a fair comparison?

These figures are illustrative, not claims about real amplifiers. The 0.005% figure is 18 times smaller numerically than 0.09%. That does not mean the music will sound 18 times cleaner, or that either level of distortion would be audible under normal conditions.

The more complete example: 80 watts per channel, 8 Ω, 20 Hz–20 kHz rated power range, ≤0.08% THD, both channels driven. This gives you a test load, output rating, frequency range and distortion ceiling, but still not a measured distortion sweep.

WHAT A 1 kHz NUMBER CANNOT TELL YOU

Important: A line showing a maximum allowed distortion is not the same thing as a plotted measurement. A one-frequency specification reveals exactly one point. A power-rating statement reveals its stated test conditions and limit, not the amplifier's full distortion spectrum.

Four things to check before comparing distortion claims

  1. How hard was the amplifier working? Was the test at 1 watt, 20 watts, rated output, or near clipping?
  2. Which notes were used? A single 1 kHz note tells you about that test point. A properly specified range can tell you more, but only if you know how it was tested.
  3. What was connected? A 4 Ω load may demand more current than 8 Ω. Stereo versus multichannel tests can also differ.
  4. What's included in the number? THD reports harmonics. THD+N includes noise as well. Measurement bandwidth and filtering also affect the reading.
One subtle trap: “20 Hz–20 kHz” may describe the full-power frequency range rather than a claim that every frequency was tested for exactly the same distortion value. Read the manufacturer’s full specification line, not just its smallest percentage.

FIRST, THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

Imagine a guitar playing a clear note. An amplifier is meant to make that electrical signal stronger, but it can also add tiny extra tones that weren't present at that level in the original.

A THD percentage reports added harmonic tones; THD+N includes noise as well. Either reading may change as the amplifier works harder, and two amplifiers with similar total values can add different kinds of extra sound. The power, speaker load and test note are important context.

Near its limits, an amplifier may stop reproducing the peaks of the signal cleanly. When the top and bottom of the waveform are restricted, this is called clipping, and the character of the added sound can change dramatically.

CONNECT THE GRAPH TO THE MUSIC

What could you actually hear?

At extremely low levels, different harmonic mixtures may not be noticeable in music at all. At greater levels, especially with simple sustained tones, coloration may become easier to hear.

The tone that seems “smooth” to one listener might seem dull or altered to another. It is safer to compare actual recordings at matched levels than to infer a listening experience from a single THD figure.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“Second harmonic is warm and musical; third harmonic is harsh.” That familiar explanation contains a useful starting idea, but it isn't a universal rule. Both added tones are musically related to the original note, and audibility depends on more than the order.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

A second harmonic of a 200 Hz note appears at 400 Hz; a third appears at 600 Hz. Their measured levels and combinations matter. The sum called THD loses information about which harmonics dominate, how the distortion changes with frequency and power, and what happens with several musical notes playing together. Tube or transistor labels do not supply that missing evidence.

ONE NUMBER, TWO POSSIBLE SOUNDS

Second versus third harmonic: what changes?

Play a clean test note at 200 Hz. If the amplifier adds a second harmonic, an extra tone appears one octave higher at 400 Hz. A third harmonic appears at 600 Hz. The percentage called THD rolls different extra tones into one figure.

A common claim is that H2 creates warmth while H3 creates hardness. That is not a universal rule: the levels, listening material, other distortion components and audible thresholds all matter. Music can also hide low-level harmonics. Purposefully added saturation in music production is a different choice from an unwanted hi-fi playback error.

TAKE THIS TO THE SHOP

Before you decide what to buy

  1. Compare figures only after checking output power, speaker load, test frequency or range, whether they report THD or THD+N, and the measurement bandwidth or filtering.
  2. Look for distortion-versus-power and distortion-versus-frequency curves, when available, instead of judging an amplifier by its smallest percentage.
  3. Treat claims about warm second-order or harsh third-order distortion as hypotheses unless an actual level and spectrum are provided.

What else can change the outcome?

Output level, frequency, speaker load, recording complexity, harmonic levels, intermodulation, amplifier circuit and feedback, ear sensitivity, and any distortion intentionally added during production.

Want the engineering explanation?Advanced detail

THD is a ratio of harmonic components to the fundamental, expressed as a percentage. THD+N also includes noise in the defined measurement bandwidth. A lone percentage doesn't specify signal frequency, power level, load impedance or measurement bandwidth, and a power-band rating is not equivalent to a complete THD-versus-frequency plot. Harmonic order is the integer multiplier of the original test tone: H2=2×, H3=3×. A conventional THD-versus-power graph commonly uses a logarithmic vertical axis; the example displayed here is entirely synthetic.
