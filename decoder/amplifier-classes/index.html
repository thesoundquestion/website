LESSON 08 · AMPLIFIERS · CIRCUIT TECHNOLOGY

Does Class A, Class AB or Class D decide how an amplifier sounds?

Learn what Class A, AB, D, G and H actually do, why people might reasonably prefer one over another, and which sound-quality claims need real evidence.

WHAT THE EXAMPLE STAT ACTUALLY MEANS

Class AB · MOSFET output stage

Illustrative, not a verified product measurement.

Read every part of the label

Class AB describes the operating method: complementary output devices share the audio signal with some overlap. MOSFET describes the kind of transistor used. Both terms can describe one amplifier, because operating class and transistor type are independent design choices.

What it doesn't establish: The label doesn't reveal the amplifier's wattage, distortion, output impedance or noise. It isn't enough to predict whether the amplifier will sound warm, clinical or transparent.

Class A

Continuous conduction, even while quiet

Class A keeps its output devices conducting across the waveform cycle. It can avoid some crossover mechanisms, but usually expends significant power as heat, even at idle.

Why people choose it: continuous conduction avoids the particular output-device handoff associated with crossover distortion. Some listeners value that design philosophy, along with the experience of owning a traditional high-bias amplifier.

Why others do not: substantial idle heat, electricity use, large heat sinks and often less practical power per dollar. A Class A label alone does not prove warmer or more accurate sound.

Some amplifiers marketed with a Class A operating mode leave that mode at higher power or under particular loads. Check the actual specification and bias conditions.

Class AB

Complementary devices share the workload

Class AB shares the waveform across devices with overlap to reduce crossover distortion. Its mix of efficiency and performance makes it common in hi-fi and home-theater equipment.

Why people choose it: a familiar linear topology balancing crossover control with better efficiency and more usable output than many pure Class A designs. A well-biased Class AB amp can be extremely clean.

Why others do not: it still wastes heat, and poor bias control or thermal design can leave crossover-related artifacts. Properly designed AB does not need to sound harsher than Class A.

Bias and measured distortion matter more than the AB badge. Some AB stages remain Class A-like for small signals, but that region depends on the actual circuit, load and bias.

Class D

Rapid switching, followed by filtering

Class D uses devices as rapid switches, typically followed by an output filter. It can be extremely efficient and accurate. The D does not stand for digital audio.

Why people choose it: high efficiency, small size, less heat, and potentially strong output from a compact or multichannel amplifier. Good modern examples can be audibly transparent.

Why others do not: the switching design introduces filter, electromagnetic-interference and feedback challenges that must be handled properly. Some designs interact with the load; some listeners simply prefer the construction or serviceability of a linear amplifier.

Class D does not mean digital sound. Never assume it sounds cold, sharp, or inferior without controlled evidence about the specific unit.

THE EVERYDAY EXPLANATION

Operating class and device type are separate choices

Operating class describes how the amplifier's output stage handles the signal. In conventional linear stages, Class A conducts throughout the cycle; Class B splits the cycle; Class AB overlaps the split to reduce crossover distortion. Class D uses high-speed switching instead.

The hardware uses specific devices. Vacuum tubes, bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) and MOSFETs describe what controls electrical current, not the amplifier class. A Class AB amplifier may use MOSFETs or BJTs. Tubes can also be used in Class A or AB designs.

Classes G and H manage the supply-rail voltages to reduce waste. They may be combined with linear output stages. Practical concerns such as cooling, reliability, output limits and power consumption often differ by design.

THE REASONS BEHIND THE PREFERENCE

Why would somebody actually choose Class A, AB, D, G or H?

There are legitimate trade-offs here. What changes most reliably is how the amplifier uses electricity, generates heat and delivers power. What you hear depends on the complete circuit, its measured behavior into your speakers, and the listening conditions. Your preference is real as an experience; the class letter alone does not explain its cause.

CLASS A · CONTINUOUS CONDUCTION

Choose it for its operating philosophy

The appeal: output devices remain conducting for the entire signal cycle. Avoiding the usual complementary handoff removes one possible crossover-distortion mechanism. Enthusiasts may like the engineering approach, a particular amplifier's measured performance or simply the equipment itself.

The compromise: inefficient at ordinary listening levels, substantial heat even at idle, more ventilation and generally a less convenient cost-to-power ratio. It does not follow that every Class A amplifier sounds warm, rich or better.

CLASS AB · A PRACTICAL MIDDLE GROUND

Choose it for clean linear power without Class A's constant losses

The appeal: complementary output devices overlap around the crossover region, reducing the dead zone of Class B while dissipating much less idle power than many Class A implementations. It can offer excellent fidelity and sensible size, heat and cost.

The compromise: bias and thermal tracking must be right, and efficiency still falls well short of many Class D designs. AB is not inherently coarse or gritty; bad crossover behavior is a design problem, not the required 'AB sound.'

CLASS D · SWITCHING EFFICIENCY

Choose it for compact, cool-running high output

The appeal: high efficiency means less waste heat and more practical output in compact amplifiers, powered speakers, subwoofers and multichannel systems. Well-executed modern Class D designs can measure extremely well.

The compromise: switching noise, output filters, load-dependent response in some designs, EMI and control-loop behavior must be engineered carefully. Those are reasons to examine the individual model, not proof that the entire class has a cold or digital tone.

CLASS G / H · SMARTER POWER-RAIL USE

Choose it when output demand varies widely

The appeal: multiple or tracking supply rails can reduce wasted energy in an amplifier that would otherwise operate with unnecessarily high supply voltage. That can help with heat and power delivery in suitable designs.

The compromise: rail-control transitions, power-supply behavior, protection and implementation complexity deserve scrutiny. G and H primarily describe rail management, and can coexist with other output-stage classes. They do not promise a sonic personality.

What about 'I prefer the way this one sounds'?

That's a valid listening preference. A particular tube or solid-state amplifier may have audible differences from another because of its distortion spectrum, output impedance, noise, frequency response, clipping behavior or interaction with speakers. Some listeners even prefer a specific, mildly colored presentation. Preference does not require the least distortion, but the class label does not prove that coloration exists or caused the preference.

For a useful comparison, match playback levels, keep the same source, speakers and placement, operate both amplifiers safely within their limits, and compare actual measurements where available. If a difference survives a controlled listening test, investigate its source rather than attributing it automatically to 'Class A warmth' or 'Class D harshness.'

An important hybrid case: Some nominally Class AB amplifiers bias their output devices so low-power operation remains in a Class A region, then transition as current demand rises. The size of that region is model- and load-dependent. A front-panel Class A switch or marketing label is not enough to establish its actual power envelope or the audibility of the transition.

WHAT MIGHT YOU HEAR?

Connect the measurements to listening

A carefully engineered Class D amplifier can reproduce music very accurately, just as a well-made Class A or AB amplifier can. The class label alone does not prove that one will have smoother guitars or punchier drums. Why might a listener prefer one? They might hear a real model-specific difference, like a particular distortion pattern or speaker interaction, or value its physical design, power delivery, efficiency and ownership experience. Those explanations should be investigated separately.

Some tube designs or other deliberately colored circuits can add audible harmonics. That coloration depends on the particular circuitry and load, not merely the presence of tubes, MOSFETs or a Class A badge.

WHAT AUDIOPHILES SAY

“I love the warmth of my Class A amp,” “Class AB sounds more natural,” “MOSFETs sound like tubes” and “Class D sounds cold” describe listener impressions worth investigating, not universal laws. A preference is meaningful; its cause remains a separate question.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

Amplifier operating class is directly related to conduction or switching behavior, efficiency and thermal design. It does not, by itself, establish output-stage linearity under real loads or a unique audible tonal character. Compare distortion-versus-power, noise, response into the speaker load, and level-matched listening results.

USE THIS WHEN BUYING

What matters in practice?

  1. Compare clean power, THD+N at relevant loads and levels, and compatibility with the speakers you actually own.
  2. Account for idle heat, ventilation and service needs; tube replacement and Class A power consumption can add ongoing costs.
  3. Read actual product measurements rather than relying on the output-device label as a substitute for sound-quality evidence. Compare THD+N over power, distortion spectra when available, frequency response with your load and clipping behavior.

Other important variables

Bias and thermal stability, output-stage feedback, supply-rail behavior, Class D output-filter behavior, noise, output power, speaker load and the measured operating point at your typical listening level. A different circuit can measure better at 2 W yet worse at 80 W.

Engineering deep dive Optional

In a conventional linear output stage, a Class A device conducts for 360° of the cycle; Class B approximately 180°; Class AB between 180° and 360°. Class D uses switching rather than the same conduction-angle classification. Class G/H implement different supply-rail management strategies.
