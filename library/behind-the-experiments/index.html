01 / MIXING

One performance, three deliberately different spaces

The Mixing Lab starts with the same 30 seconds of music (0:40–1:10) from 14 separate recorded parts, called stems. These are processed-dry parts, not untouched microphone recordings. We made three mixes and adjusted each finished example to the same measured average loudness, −20 LUFS. That makes it easier to compare the creative differences without being swayed by one mix simply playing louder. We did not apply mastering compression or limiting to these versions.

Side-to-mid level ratio of the three teaching mixes. A less negative number means more stereo side energy relative to the center, not necessarily a better mix.
MixSide / midWhat the treatment illustrates
Extreme narrow−42.77 dBAn intentionally near-mono presentation with much of the sound gathered near the center
Conventional reference−14.61 dBA more natural stereo distribution using the same recorded performance
Creative / polished−9.68 dBDeliberately theatrical width, motion, ambience and arrangement emphasis

These numbers tell us how much more sound energy is sent toward the sides than toward the centre of the stereo picture. They cannot tell us how far back an instrument seems, whether individual parts are easier to follow, or which mix sounds best. We changed several things at once, including left-right placement (panning), echo/room effects and automated moves. So this is a listening demonstration, not a controlled test of just one setting.

Why playback can change the sense of space

Stereo width and front-to-back depth are not the same thing. A mix may have clearly identifiable left/right placement while its apparent depth changes with the listening setup. Headphones send a separate signal to each ear, while loudspeakers interact with the room. Placement, reflections and listening position can influence perceived depth, centre imaging and how spatial movement is heard.

For this demonstration, headphones may make some stereo cues easier to follow, but neither playback method is a universal reference for every spatial effect. Comparing headphones and speakers at comfortable levels can illustrate an important principle: the recorded mix is only one part of the listening experience. This is a general explanation, not a conclusion from a controlled headphone-versus-speaker study.

02 / MASTERING

Same rough mix. Different finishing decisions.

Our mastering comparison is built around one shared rough mix. That matters: if you change the mix between examples, listeners cannot know whether a missing triangle, a buried guitar or a changed vocal balance was caused by mastering or by a different mix.

FULL DYNAMIC

Keep the movement

Preserve greater contrast between quiet details and musical peaks, rather than chasing maximum average loudness.

MODERATE MASTER

Some control, some punch

Use a more restrained combination of level, equalization and dynamic control to show a middle ground.

BRICKWALLED

Push it far enough to hear

Extreme processing reduces the contrast between sharp musical hits and the sound around them. That lets you hear the trade-off when making the whole song consistently loud becomes the priority.

These are teaching extremes, not claims that every loud master is bad or every dynamic version sounds best. A bright percussion accent, such as the triangle in the rough mix, is useful to listen for. Does its attack stand out, get masked, or seem to change its place among the other instruments?

How to judge the comparison fairly

Listen two ways. At their actual mastered levels, you hear the practical loudness difference. At approximately matched listening loudness, you can pay closer attention to punch, distortion, tonal balance and whether quieter details are easier to follow. Louder often seems more exciting by itself, so the two listening modes answer different questions.

Look at integrated loudness (average level over the passage), peak and true-peak level (how close the strongest moments get to the ceiling), and measures of peak-to-average contrast. No single meter gives a universal score for musical quality. If EQ changes as well as compression, don't attribute every tonal difference to compression alone.

03 / MP3 VS FLAC

The same recording. Three different file versions.

Our MP3 vs Lossless Lab asks a narrower question than “Which format is best?”: when you keep the music and master fixed, can you hear what MP3 encoding changes? We start with two existing educational FLAC recordings. FLAC is lossless, meaning decoding reconstructs the source audio without discarding sound information. MP3 makes a smaller file by removing or simplifying information according to its encoder. The format names alone cannot tell you whether an individual listener will hear a difference.

What recordings did we actually use?

The same two source performances are used for the lossless reference and both MP3 versions of each example.

Both performances come from music made available through AlbumDB, but the two source recordings are our already-prepared educational versions. Each MP3 was encoded directly from the corresponding FLAC. We did not record another take, change the balance of instruments, apply new EQ, or remix or remaster the files for this particular format comparison.

How we made and checked the MP3 versions

We encoded both sources with FFmpeg 8.0.1 using the LAME MP3 encoder, at the source's native 44.1 kHz stereo rate. For each source we made one 128 kbps and one 320 kbps constant-bitrate (CBR) file. Bitrate is the amount of encoded data used per second. We checked the decoded duration, sample alignment, sample-level RMS and peak level, and recorded SHA-256 file hashes in our format-test provenance manifest.

The two 128 kbps files decode to an RMS level about 0.44 dB lower than their FLAC originals. This matters because even a small volume difference can influence a casual listening judgement. The 320 kbps versions measured almost identical sample RMS to their sources. Zero-sample alignment and high signal correlation are useful engineering checks; they are not measurements of whether the differences are audible or musically important.

What happens when you press Play?

For the normal listening controls, the browser plays one version on demand and switches to approximately the same point in the other file. To reduce the measured level advantage of the lossless and 320 kbps files, the player applies playback-only volume multipliers of roughly 0.951 to those two versions; the 128 kbps file stays at 1.0. The stored recordings are unchanged. This is a practical, approximate sample-RMS match, not a calibrated perceived-loudness match.

An optional Prepare instant switching control downloads and decodes all three versions of the chosen song. Where supported, a shared Web Audio clock starts them at the same time and playback position; the buttons then change their gain rather than restarting the files. Preparation is deliberately opt-in, with a 14 MiB total download cap and a 48 MiB decoded-audio limit. It uses additional memory and bandwidth. If preparation fails or the browser cannot decode the FLAC, ordinary audio playback remains available, but normal switching may pause or drift slightly in position.

What can we conclude?

These tests demonstrate that different encodings of the same source can be compared, and they document the actual encoding and level differences. If someone hears no difference, that is a perfectly useful personal observation. If they do hear one, a blind comparison can help check whether it survives without a visible format label. Neither result from one person, two excerpts or one playback setup proves what all listeners can hear. Encoding quality, musical material, listening conditions and replay level all matter.

04 / ABX BLIND LISTENING

When the label disappears, can you still tell?

Our ABX Lab adds a different question to the sighted Mixing, Mastering and MP3 comparisons. You can hear known references A and B as many times as you need. A mystery X is one of them. You identify whether X matches A or B. The aim is to separate an audible difference you can repeatedly identify from a preference or expectation caused by seeing which version is playing.

What is actually tested?

The deliberately unequal-volume mastering examples are not ABX score tests: it would be too easy to identify which was playing merely by loudness. The blind MP3 games reuse the same fixed FLAC and MP3 sources documented above, including the small decoded level difference of the 128 kbps files. Training applies a compensating playback level for those comparisons, but scored playback must not be mistaken for a calibrated laboratory test.

How do the three modes work?

01

Training: ten practice rounds

Choose a comparison and explicitly prepare the recordings. The browser randomly chooses X as A or B for each round and gives immediate feedback. When supported, it uses two decoded source buffers sharing one audio clock so switching A, B and X does not require new downloads or a restart. When synchronized decoding is unavailable, regular cached audio may introduce a pause. Training results stay on the device and are not published.

02

Challenge: ten rounds

Eligible 128 kbps format comparisons use ten server-scored guesses on the canonical site. You can replay A, B and X, but you are not told whether a guess was right until the session ends. The mastering EQ challenge remains a private practice game because the separate server-scored playback path has not passed its fairness checks.

03

Advanced: twenty rounds

Eligible 320 kbps lossless-versus-MP3 comparisons use twenty guesses. This provides a longer run with a subtler encoding comparison. It does not certify anyone's hearing or guarantee that the material contains an audible difference.

How do we prevent the browser from awarding itself points?

The scored service, rather than the web page, chooses which underlying file corresponds to A and B, determines X for each round, and issues a round-specific token. It checks the current answer and advances the score only once, so resubmitting a guess cannot count it again. The browser receives the final correct-answer count and a chance probability, calculated as the probability of that many correct guesses or more if each two-choice guess were random. That calculation does not account for repeated attempts, a self-selected audience, different equipment or uncontrolled listening conditions.

Scored audio is played through a separate authenticated delivery route and cached for the session. However, the scored player still uses normal browser media switching. Network timing, playback gaps, codec characteristics, small level differences or someone deliberately inspecting downloaded files can compromise blinding. The service hides the answer and avoids revealing results between rounds, but this is an informal listening game, not a cheat-proof or scientifically controlled hearing study.

What happens to the scores?

On the canonical site, choosing to include a completed result in anonymous community statistics is optional and off by default. Results are grouped by the specific versioned comparison, game mode and trial count. A group is not shown publicly until at least 10 completed, opted-in sessions exist. These are counts of sessions, not unique people, and there are no individual rankings. The score database does not require an account, name or email. Unfinished sessions expire after two hours; completed private results are scheduled for deletion after 24 hours, and opted-in results after 180 days, with a scheduled cleanup job.

Preview safety: On this draft preview, Challenge and Advanced run as private practice only. No public score submission occurs. That is intentional; successful preview playback alone does not verify the whole live scoring chain.

05 / THE METHOD

What makes a listening comparison useful?

01

Hold the right things constant

Use the same performance, time range and starting file when testing a specific production decision. Label any exceptions rather than hiding them.

02

Distinguish gain matching from mastering

Our three mixing examples were gain-matched for listening; that operation did not make them separately mastered records. For mastering, report the chosen gain and dynamic treatment of each version.

03

Let your ears and the measurements ask different questions

A stereo ratio tells us about side energy. A loudness meter tells us about level. Listening impressions can describe spaciousness or punch. They support one another, but none replaces the others.

04

Report limitations

Our deliberately exaggerated mixes and masters, and the guided format switching, are teaching demonstrations. ABX helps reduce some expectation effects, but unsupervised games still have level, playback, participant-selection and procedural limitations. Do not turn a single score into proof of universal audibility.
