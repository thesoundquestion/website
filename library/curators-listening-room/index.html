THE SOUND QUESTION / LISTENING RECOMMENDATIONS

The Curator's Listening Room.

Some songs grab you with a great riff. Others pull you in with the groove, the atmosphere, or the way the musicians play off one another. And every once in a while, everything comes together.

These aren't necessarily the best-recorded songs out there. They're recordings I've found interesting, enjoyable, or downright impressive for different reasons. Some sound particularly good on a decent stereo system or headphones. Others just have something about the music that keeps me coming back. These are listening suggestions, not homework: your favourite songs matter more than any list of supposedly perfect-sounding recordings, including mine.

When the Stars Align

The ones where the riffs, rhythm, dynamics and musical development all seem to come together.

Tool

Lateralus

Probably one of my favourite examples of how complicated rhythms can still feel completely natural. There's a hypnotic riff holding everything together while the drums and other instruments seem to be doing their own thing. Eventually, it all comes together. That's the part I love.

The Tea Party

Sister Awake

Find on Spotify ↗

One of my all-time favourites. It's got that heavy, hypnotic groove, some fantastic guitar work, and all these different instrumental textures working together. The song keeps building and changing without losing what made it interesting in the first place.

Porcupine Tree

Anesthetize

Find on Spotify ↗

A long song that actually earns its length. The middle section in particular has this massive, driving riff with some fantastic drumming underneath it. There's a lot going on, but it still has a groove you can latch onto. It moves through several different moods and gives you a real payoff.

I Mother Earth

So Gently We Go

Find on Spotify ↗

A great example of a song that takes you somewhere. There's a lot of percussion, interesting guitar work and changes in intensity, but it never feels like the band is just showing off. Each section adds something to the overall experience.

Rush

Xanadu

Find on Spotify ↗

This one has pretty much everything I enjoy in a longer song. A patient buildup, memorable guitar parts, a rhythm section with plenty going on, and changes in intensity that actually serve the music. It's progressive rock that feels like a complete piece of music rather than a collection of complicated parts.

There's a great combination of heavy guitar, percussion and changing rhythms here. The song has plenty of energy, but it's the Latin marching band feel, mixed with the tribal groove and gritty vocals that really makes it interesting.

Tool

Disposition / Reflection / Triad

Find on Spotify ↗

These three tracks really belong together. They start with a hypnotic, almost meditative feel, gradually introduce more layers and rhythms, and eventually become something much heavier. I particularly enjoy how the individual rhythmic parts interact without losing the underlying pulse. It's a long listen, but if you've got the time and space for it... chefs kiss

Listen in sequence: Disposition → Reflection → Triad.

Tool

The Patient

Find on Spotify ↗

This is the kind of slow buildup I enjoy. It starts relatively restrained, gradually introduces more weight, and has some excellent riffs along the way. Listen to the cymbals, too: the individual patterns and accents stay clearly defined instead of becoming a wash, while the extended, airy top end lets the smaller hits shine through. The changes feel earned, and that high-frequency detail is one of the recording's real highlights.

Tool

No Quarter

Find on Spotify ↗

I love what Tool did with this song. It has a much thicker, heavier feel while retaining the atmosphere and recognizable musical ideas. The way it moves between quieter passages and heavier sections makes it a great journey from beginning to end. And the Drum fills? Yeah....

Dream Theater

The Count of Tuscany

Find on Spotify ↗

Another sprawling song with heavy riffs, quieter passages and a wonderful sense of return. The lyrics make it even better: Petrucci based them on a real trip with friends to a Tuscan winery and castle, where eerie family stories and strange cellar visits had everyone joking about whether they'd get out alive. He then turned an innocent, goofy experience into a 19-minute prog-rock near-horror story. The narrator's paranoia almost sounds like somebody had a little too much 'herb' (my joke, not Petrucci's explanation). A brilliantly over-the-top way to turn a personal adventure into an epic.

The real-life backstory: Petrucci's 2009 interview with Guitar Messenger ↗.

Boston

Foreplay / Long Time

Boston (1976) · Original studio version

Find on Spotify ↗

Epic, from the instrumental opening to the huge guitar sound. Tom Scholz was a genius in more ways than one. And it's a good reminder that a heavily compressed rock sound can still be spectacular when it serves the music.

The Proclaimers

I'm Gonna Be (500 Miles) / I'm on My Way

Sunshine on Leith (1988) · Original studio recordings

Find on Spotify ↗

Put these two songs on back to back and listen to how differently the instruments seem to sit in the mix.

On I'm Gonna Be (500 Miles), the vocals and guitars sound more prominent to my ears. The drums seem more tightly controlled, possibly more compressed, but the cymbals and other high-frequency detail still come through nicely. It's punchy and surprisingly open without everything blending into a wall of sound.

Then switch to I'm on My Way. To my ears, this mix feels a little more open, with the vocals and guitars sitting farther back and more room around the individual instruments. It has a different sense of space, despite coming from the same album.

That doesn't necessarily mean one recording was compressed more than the other. Microphone placement, the recording room, instrument balance, EQ, compression or other mixing decisions could all be contributing to the difference.

Back-to-back listening recommendation: Play them at roughly the same volume and pay particular attention to the drum tone, cymbals, vocal placement and space around the guitars. It's a neat example of how two recordings from the same album can feel noticeably different without either one sounding bad. Find I'm on My Way on Spotify ↗.

Mixing & Production Choices

Recordings where the way everything has been put together is part of what makes them interesting.

↑ Categories

Los Lobos

Dream in Blue

Find on Spotify ↗

This is a great example of what a good mix can do for a song. There are a lot of interesting sounds and rhythmic textures, but everything seems to have its own place. The instruments remain distinct without the recording feeling disconnected. There's a real sense of space and individuality to the different parts.

Blind Melon

Soak the Sin / Tones of Home

Blind Melon (1992) · Opening two tracks · Original studio recordings

Find song on Spotify ↗

Back to back, these two songs are a great example of a busy rock arrangement that still sounds remarkably open to my ears. There's plenty happening between the guitars, bass and drums, but it never turns into a wall of sound. The drum tone is fantastic, and the cymbals and other high-end detail come through clearly without taking over the mix. Lots of energy, but you can still follow what each musician is doing.

These were the opening two tracks on one of my first favourite albums, so they've always had a place with me. Blind Melon are mostly remembered for No Rain, which is ironically just about the only song you ever hear. I like it, but to my ears almost every other track on this album outdoes it. That one hit never came close to showing what this band could do.

Full-album recommendation: If No Rain is all you've heard, start with Soak the Sin, let Tones of Home play, and keep going. Give the entire Blind Melon (1992) album a proper front-to-back listen. Listen to the full album on Spotify ↗

Spatial Effects & QSound

These are the recordings that made me pay attention to where sounds seemed to be coming from, rather than just what was being played.

↑ Categories

Yosi Horikawa

Bubbles

Find on Spotify ↗

A really interesting recording for headphones and a system/room capable of a sense of width. There are all these little percussive sounds moving around, and some seem to occupy very specific positions. It's almost as much about the textures and placement of the sounds as it is about the music.

Another interesting use of QSound. The groove stays pretty well anchored while different sounds seem to move around a much larger space. It's a fun demonstration of just how convincing a two-speaker recording can be.

The spatial effects on this album are pretty remarkable, and this is one of the songs I actually enjoy musically as well. Some of the sounds seem to come from places well outside the speakers. It's a great combination of interesting production and a riff/groove worth listening to.

A note about playback

Most of these songs can be enjoyed on just about anything. A good riff or groove doesn't stop being good because you're listening on inexpensive equipment.

That said, some recordings have subtle details, instrument separation or spatial effects that are easier to appreciate on a decent pair of headphones or a properly set up stereo system. You don't need expensive equipment, but a little attention to how you're listening can make a surprising difference.

These are personal listening picks, not equipment tests. Song links generally open Spotify searches; a few full-album recommendations link directly to albums. Neither guarantees a particular master. Wherever possible, look for the original studio recording rather than a remaster or live version; availability can vary.
