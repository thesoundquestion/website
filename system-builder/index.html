02

Which route fits your space?

A working setup needs more than a promising speaker model. Passive speakers need an amplifier. Powered speakers have amplification built in, but check whether the price covers both speakers and whether you need a volume control or other connections.

Open styles leak sound. Closed and in-ear designs can help with isolation, but real fit and frequency response are model-specific. We do not rank headphones from price or impedance alone.

What do these listening levels actually feel like?

Think of the music at your listening chair, not the volume knob position. These approximate references describe average sound level (dBA), not a precise setting for every stereo or recording:

  • 60–70 dBA: easy conversation. Music is present, but you can generally talk to somebody beside you without competing with it. Ordinary conversation is often around 60 dBA.
  • Around 80 dBA: quite loud at home. Think of the sound level of a nearby vacuum cleaner or a busy kitchen appliance. Normal speech becomes harder to follow over the music.
  • Around 85 dBA: loud for sustained listening. Some hair dryers or blenders reach this region nearby. You will probably raise your voice to talk. Duration matters to hearing risk.
  • 110 dB brief peak: a very different number. This describes a potentially extremely loud, short musical hit, not an 110 dBA listening average. Do not turn up your stereo to try to reproduce it.

Appliance examples vary substantially by model and distance. A-rated average levels, instantaneous peak SPL and electrical amplifier headroom are different quantities; the Builder's three choices do not calculate or certify a particular dB value.

Try a rough sound-level check with your phone
  1. Use an SPL / sound-level app that shows dBA and preferably LAeq (the average sound energy over a period). The free NIOSH Sound Level Meter is an iPhone/iPad example. On Android, NoiseCapture is an available option, but device accuracy varies.
  2. In your actual listening room, put the phone at about seated ear height where you listen, with its microphone unobstructed. If testing elsewhere, note that distance and room change the result.
  3. Play familiar music and choose a comfortable listening level. Read the LAeq over 30–60 seconds or longer, not just a flickering instantaneous number. Do not increase volume simply to reach a target dB.
  4. If the app shows maximum or peak, note it separately; it is not the same as the average, and an ordinary phone microphone can clip or miss fast, loud transients.

Weighting made simple: A-weighting (dBA) reduces the influence of very low and high frequencies and is commonly used to describe hearing exposure. C-weighting (dBC) reduces bass much less, while Z-weighting is nominally flat. A LAeq average is more useful than a single jumping number for getting a rough feel for your usual music level.

Know the limit: Phone microphones, cases and automatic processing differ, especially on Android. Without calibration this is a rough guide, not a certified SPL or safe-exposure measurement. It is also not a reliable way to verify a 110 dB transient, headphone/earbud output at your eardrum, or a speaker's maximum clean output.

For an actual amplifier power/headroom estimate, use System Check with documented speaker and amplifier specifications. An app's dBA average is not automatically the same as its unweighted dB SPL target, and the assumed peak gap still needs separate consideration.

Reference examples and measurement guidance: CDC/NIOSH measurement guidance · WHO listening duration · NIOSH measuring sound

How will the subwoofer connect?

These are ordinary socket types. Choose “Not sure” when you do not know. We will not assume that having an RCA input means your amplifier has an RCA output.

A Sub Out usually uses an RCA lead; a speaker-level connection requires extra speaker wire and an input designed for it. A subwoofer crossover knob normally filters the subwoofer, not your existing speakers. Understand subwoofer setup.

How these examples are chosen and what the limitations are

Facts, not vibes

Equipment preset claims link to their source where available. Sensitivity bases, load ratings, peak versus continuous output, and frequency-response tolerances matter.

Unknown is not a zero

Unmeasured directivity, clean bass output, room behaviour or used pricing is neither a failing score nor a hidden advantage.

We link, not copy

Independent reviews are useful for checking how real equipment behaves. We link to original measurements, explain their conditions, and keep the reviewer’s work at its source. We do not scrape or reconstruct restricted measurement databases, and a source link does not imply a partnership. See our content-use approach.
