# The Sound Question — Musical Easter Eggs: Cross-Media Editorial Standard
**Established:** 2026-11-19  
**Status:** Owner-approved creative direction. Editorial convention, not authorization to publish, deploy, use copyrighted recordings, or quote lyrics.

## Why this is part of the brand

The Sound Question is **music first, questions and evidence in service of the listening experience**. Readers should feel encouraged to play the records they already love. A song need not be an audiophile demonstration, impeccably recorded or even technically "good" to be worth listening to. High-quality recordings can be revealing learning examples, but should never be treated as compulsory listening.

The Easter eggs invite people to discover more of the site by rewarding their own knowledge and musical taste. Recognizable popular references make the first discovery accessible; deeper cuts are scattered naturally across relevant lessons. Do not turn this into a scored hunt, publish a spoiler map, or announce or highlight the references. The reader's enjoyment of music remains the reason for exploring the specifications.

## Intent
Let the reader or viewer occasionally discover a musical reference that belongs naturally in the explanation. References should be unannounced, unhighlighted, and amusing to those who recognize them without confusing those who do not. They are a quiet sign that the project is written by listeners, not a substitute for useful technical explanations.

**Apply across:** website lessons, glossaries, system tools and results text, listening labs, the Curator's Listening Room, reference guides, YouTube long-form scripts, Shorts, voiceovers, captions, podcasts, descriptions and future educational content. A video and its adapted site article need not reuse the same reference.

## Source pool
- **Represent the reader's music tastes, not only the site's owner's personal listening preferences.** Draw naturally from pop, hip-hop, R&B, soul, country, folk, dance, rock, metal, alternative, jazz and music across eras and generations. Neither the owner's collection nor the Curator's Listening Room should determine what genres can appear.
- The Curator's Listening Room can inspire examples when a specific track fits, but it is **not a gatekeeper** for Easter-egg selection. A well-fitting reference from outside that collection is preferable to a forced reference from inside it.
- Search broadly across **popular songs, albums, band/performer names, recognizable lyrical themes, and original wordplay or puns**, not just song-title substitutions. A reference may use an artist's name, a familiar title transformed into a technical pun, or an original technical analogy inspired by a song's idea. Do not reproduce or closely paraphrase copyrighted lyrics. The recognizable reference must also serve the audio explanation.
- **Popular and widely recognizable references come first.** Across all genres, prefer songs, performers, albums and musical ideas that a substantial, varied readership is likely to recognize. Do not search for obscure material merely to fill a genre or justify a clever pun. Lesser-known references are optional only when exceptionally natural; popularity does not excuse a forced or technically misleading insertion.
- Diversity is a quality check across the full curriculum, **not a genre quota per page**. A country reference should not appear just to tick a box; nor should an obvious hip-hop, pop or soul reference be rejected because the author would not personally play it.
- Identify the intended artist/title accurately in the **private editorial ledger**, especially where titles or similar phrases are shared by multiple songs. The public copy need not identify the reference. If owner attribution is unclear, record it as unconfirmed instead of guessing.

## Reader-facing discovery standard

These references exist to make dry audio lessons fun to explore, not to be invisible. The owner wants readers to recognize familiar music while they learn. Work several approachable, unhighlighted references into a normal-length lesson where the subject naturally permits; strong candidates include the introductory hook, a technical analogy that really helps, the listening example and, in selected lessons, an actual lesson title. Use a wide range of accessible musical touchstones, including pop, hip-hop, soul, country and other styles, rather than repeatedly relying on one era or the owner's preferred artists.

A merely music-themed sentence isn't a song Easter egg. Before logging a placement, identify its real song title/artist internally and check that the phrase is distinctive enough to catch without the hidden ledger. It must still read naturally and technically accurately to someone who doesn't recognize it. Vary eras and styles; quality takes priority over numerical quotas. Never reproduce song lyrics or create hidden links that spoil the game.

## Reference selection: narrative first, recognition second

1. Identify the actual teaching point first: clipping, signal-chain compatibility, amplifier headroom, speaker dispersion, room effects, recording, coding or a listening decision.
2. Brainstorm **widely known** musical associations from many genres and decades. Explore multiple forms: artist/band names, album names, recognizable song titles, transformed titles, an original line with a meaningful double meaning, or an original analogy suggested by a lyrical concept.
3. Choose the candidate that fits the **sentence and lesson** most naturally. It should be recognizable *and* useful. A famous reference that distracts from the point is worse than an ordinary clear explanation.
4. Preserve the approved lesson titles and their short explanatory bridges. Where a different popular reference fits deeper in a lesson, add it without repeating an already-used musical hook.
5. Check the private editorial ledger for duplication and note the precise song/album/artist inspiration. References not yet written into the actual site are **candidates**, not active placements.

Do not constrain the search to the owner's favorite artists, a prescribed genre distribution or a finite shortlist of songs. The aim is broad musical familiarity across the audience, not obscure-reference hunting.

## Short musical breathers in technical lessons

**Purpose: give an overloaded reader a short mental break without breaking the lesson.** Song-title, artist-name and other recognizable musical allusions are seasoning, not the meal. The bulk of every lesson stays straightforward, accurate, beginner-friendly instruction.

- **Where they belong:** the occasional lesson opening, a short mid-lesson example after a dense explanation or graph, a relatable anecdote, a listening comparison or a natural transition. Insert where the reader benefits from a pause, not at fixed intervals.
- **How long:** usually a single sentence or a brief two-to-four-sentence paragraph. On occasion, two compatible references can share a small scene, but avoid extended song-collage narratives or turning full lessons into connected-reference stories. No numerical quota or mandatory musical break for every section.
- **What they accomplish:** restate one technical point in familiar terms, make an abstract claim tangible, or remind the reader why the specification matters for listening. After the breather, return directly to the technical topic. A reader who misses every allusion still gets a useful example.
- **How they sound:** natural, unannounced prose, drawing on broadly recognizable songs, performer names, bands, titles and original puns from any genre. No labels explaining the joke and no decorative boxes just to announce an Easter egg.
- **Editorial discipline:** write original examples, not commercial lyric passages or close paraphrases. Never sacrifice a definition, safety note, measurement caveat or explanation for the joke. Review pacing aloud and remove an allusion if it distracts or repeats an existing reference.
- **No overuse:** a long technical section with no musical references is fine. Place a few memorable moments where they refresh attention, instead of trying to pack every paragraph with references. The site's teaching and practical examples remain the primary content.

## How to write them
1. Draft the accurate, beginner-friendly explanation or script **without** the Easter egg first.
2. Find a naturally compatible sentence: e.g. signal path, a gear trade-off, comparing levels, a room problem, setup choices, or a listening impression.
3. Use original wordplay, an unobtrusive title phrase, or a band-name nod. **Do not paste or closely paraphrase copyrighted lyric lines** into site copy, scripts, captions or graphic text without separately clearing rights. An allusion is not permission to use a lyric or song recording.
4. Keep normal typography and narration. No quotation marks, italics, highlighted labels, emoji callouts, hover clues, footnotes explaining the reference, or public Easter-egg index.
5. Run the sentence by itself: a first-time visitor must understand the lesson even if the song reference means nothing to them. Technical correctness, the site's beginner-first rules, and clarity always win.
6. Delete rather than force a weak pun, repetition, tonal mismatch or reference that interrupts a serious/sensitive passage. No fixed quota.
7. Maintain an internal map of placements, source inspirations and status; distinguish *proposed*, *draft branch*, *merged*, and *actually deployed/published*. Never assume a GitHub commit is on Cloudflare.
8. Avoid unauthorized music playback, commercial audio clips, cover art or claims of artist endorsement. Those require separate rights and provenance review.


## Lesson title and opening paragraph rule
- The **lesson overview, directory cards and Previous/Next links** use descriptive technical subject names, not musical puns. Search/browser metadata should prioritize the technical topic.
- The **individual lesson H1** may use an owner-approved musical title, preferably with a clear technical descriptor.
- The **first explanatory paragraph directly below the H1** must bridge the title to the technical subject. A reader unfamiliar with the song should still understand why the heading fits; the paragraph must immediately teach something useful rather than explain the reference or apologize for the pun.
- Do not name the artist, identify the lyric, label the joke, or force the reference a second time. Avoid gratuitous rhyme and sacrifice neither factual accuracy nor beginner clarity.
- Audit the H1/lead pairing whenever a musical title changes; ensure mobile layouts and the lesson overview remain coherent.
- When a selected lesson title adopts a song already used elsewhere, keep the strongest educational placement and remove the weaker duplicate. Treat a title plus its natural opening explanation as one placement, not two. Keep cross-lesson Previous/Next labels technical, and reconcile the private ledger after every batch of title changes.

**Approved examples**: “Chains Keep It Together” opens by walking the signal through source, conversion and amplification; “Over the Ears and Far Away” opens with fit, cup design and leakage; “Born to Be Loud” contrasts concert PA output with nearfield and living-room uses.

## Script-writing / video-specific check
- **Long form:** A few well-placed lines can reward attentive viewers; avoid running gags and scripted-sounding references. Read aloud and cut any line that sounds rehearsed or derails the user's natural speaking style. Leave room for ad-libs.
- **Shorts:** Usually one or none; the hook, demonstration and takeaway have priority. A reference must not consume the seconds needed to explain the concept.
- **On-screen text / subtitles:** Let captions reflect natural narration. Do not animate or highlight the Easter egg, and never attach copyrighted lyrics as background text.
- **Titles, thumbnails and metadata:** Avoid presenting someone else's lyric or an artist's name as the video’s own core promise, or implying an affiliation. A subtle line in narration is preferable to bait in the title.
- **Final script handoff:** Record the proposed reference, intended source, exact line/location, spoken-flow result, technical/copyright review, and its status, then keep the publicly released script free of behind-the-scenes annotation.

## Placement tracking without spoiling the Easter eggs

The November 19 consolidated draft has **103 tracked musical-reference placements across 39 website source pages** after the title-and-body deduplication audit (14 historical placements retired; 13 uniquely attributable title/artist references added). These are review-branch source, not merged or deployed; this is not a live-site count. A **private placement ledger in `thesoundquestion/research`** tracks individual source songs, draft lines, and page locations; do not expose that inventory in site pages or public materials, since it would reveal the Easter eggs. Continue to reject lines that weaken technical explanations or sound contrived; the count is not a quota.

## Editorial release checklist
- Did the technical meaning survive unchanged?
- Does the reference fit the immediate teaching point or spoken cadence?
- Is the line understandable without musical knowledge?
- Is the source an allusion/title rather than an uncleared lyric or audio excerpt?
- Is it already used too often nearby?
- Does the draft remain visually unmarked and accessible?
- Is the source/placement tracked privately and the public-release status truthful?
